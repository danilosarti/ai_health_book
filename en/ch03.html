How to use this chapter

Starting here?

This chapter leans on a few ideas from Chapter 1.

  • A dataset has \(n\) instances (rows, here patients), predictors \(\mathbf{x}_i\) and an outcome \(y_i\) (Definition 1.7). A model estimates the unknown relationship \(y = f(\mathbf{x}) + \varepsilon\); its prediction is \(\hat y = \hat f(\mathbf{x})\) (Definition 1.3).
  • The predictive pipeline fits a model on a training set, checks it on a test set it has never seen, and only then deploys it to new patients (Section 1.8).
  • A model that is too rigid has high bias (it underfits); one that is too flexible has high variance and overfits: excellent on its training data, poor on new data (Definition 1.8).
  • \(V\)-fold cross-validation rotates a held-out fold through the training data to estimate the error on new data without touching the test set (Definition 1.9).
  • A prediction serves a decision; on Ward 7 the question is whether to start a kidney-protective bundle, and a rise in creatinine of at least 0.3 mg/dL in 48 hours defines acute kidney injury (AKI) (Section 1.5).

3.1 The workflow at a glance

Chapter 1 described every learning method by the same anatomy: data go into an algorithm, which produces a model whose performance we measure. Figure 3.1 adds the three ideas that decide whether that measured performance can be trusted. Model bias attaches to the algorithm: its assumptions may be too rigid for the data. Overfitting attaches to performance: a model can look excellent on the patients it learned from and disappoint on everyone else. And cross-validation links the two: it is the procedure that tells us, before deployment, how the model is likely to perform on patients it has not seen.

In practice the workflow has six steps (Kuhn and Johnson 2013; Hastie, Tibshirani, and Friedman 2009, ch. 7), and the rest of the chapter takes them one at a time:

  1. Split the data into a training set and a test set, and lock the test set away (Section 3.2).
  2. Choose a metric that reflects what an error costs (Section 3.3).
  3. Fit a baseline: the simplest sensible prediction, which every model must beat (Section 3.4).
  4. Estimate performance with cross-validation on the training set (Section 3.5), checking at every step that no information leaks from the evaluation data into the model (Section 3.6).
  5. Tune the model’s settings, its hyperparameters, with cross-validation; our first flexible learner, \(k\)-nearest neighbours, has one (Section 3.7, Section 3.8).
  6. Open the test set once, report the error, and only then deploy.

3.2 Training, validation and test sets

A model is useful only if it generalises: if it predicts well for patients who were not used to build it. Because tomorrow’s patients are not available today, we imitate them by holding some of today’s patients back.

Definition 3.1 (Training, validation and test sets) The training set is the part of the data used to fit a model, that is, to estimate its parameters. The validation set is used to compare candidate models or settings and choose among them. The test set is kept aside until all choices have been made and is used once, to estimate how the chosen model will perform on new data. A model generalises when its error on new data is close to its error on the data it was trained on. In cross-validation the validation sets are carved out of the training set in turn, so no separate validation set is needed.

Why three roles and not two? Because every time we look at a set of patients to make a choice, that set stops being an honest judge of the choice. Chapter 1 showed this with the polynomial widget: picking the degree with the lowest test error made that test error too optimistic. A student who sits the same mock exam until they pass has learned the mock exam; the real exam must be one they have not seen (James et al. 2021, ch. 5; Hastie, Tibshirani, and Friedman 2009, ch. 7).

For Ward 7 the book uses one fixed split, stored in data/splits/ward7_split.csv and created by a script with a fixed seed (Figure 3.2). Of the 1 250 past patients, 1000 form the training set, divided at random into five folds of 200 for cross-validation, and 250 form the test set. The share of patients who met the AKI criterion is similar in the two parts (58 % in training, 59 % in test), as a random split should give. Every model of Part II is fitted on the same 1 000 patients and judged on the same 250, so that their errors can be compared fairly from chapter to chapter. ONCO-1 has its own fixed split (800 training, 200 test) in data/splits/onco1_split.csv.

A random split answers a narrow question: how well does the model do on more patients like these, from the same hospitals and the same period? A clinician deciding whether to trust a tool needs more. Temporal validation tests on patients treated later than the training patients; internal-external validation leaves out one hospital at a time; external validation tests in a different setting altogether, ideally by an independent team (Steyerberg and Harrell 2016; Steyerberg 2019). Reporting guidelines for clinical prediction models ask authors to say exactly which of these was done (Collins et al. 2015). The difference is not academic: a deep learning model for pneumonia on chest X-rays performed well on new images from the hospitals it was trained on and worse at another hospital system; the same kind of network could also tell which hospital system an image came from, a shortcut that does not transfer (Zech et al. 2018). Tested on whose patients? is always a fair question; we put it to our own Ward 7 model in Section 3.8.

A team compares ten models on the Ward 7 test set, picks the one with the smallest test error and reports that error in its paper. What is wrong?

3.3 Measuring error: metrics for regression

Before we can compare models we need to agree on how to score them. When the outcome is a number, such as the change in creatinine on Ward 7 or tumour shrinkage in ONCO-1, the task is called regression and the scores are built from the individual prediction errors. Each metric captures a different aspect of performance, and the choice should reflect what a wrong prediction costs in practice.

The error for instance \(i\) is the difference between what happened and what was predicted,

\[ e_i = y_i - \hat y_i , \qquad(3.1)\]

so a positive error means the model under-estimated and a negative error means it over-estimated. (Some texts define the error the other way round, \(\hat y_i - y_i\); only the sign changes. We use \(y_i - \hat y_i\) throughout the book, the same convention as for the residuals of Chapter 4.)

Table 3.1 follows eight patients of a chemotherapy trial, whose observed tumour shrinkage is compared with a model’s prediction.

The mean absolute error averages the size of the errors, ignoring their sign:

\[ \text{MAE} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \lvert y_i - \hat y_i \rvert . \qquad(3.2)\]

Every error counts in proportion to its size. In Table 3.1, MAE = 26 / 8 = 3.25: the predictions are, on average, 3.25 percentage points away from the observed shrinkage.

The mean squared error averages the squared errors:

\[ \text{MSE} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \hat y_i)^2 . \qquad(3.3)\]

Squaring penalises large errors much more than small ones: an error of 4 contributes 16, an error of 8 contributes 64. That is useful when a big miss is disproportionately costly, for example when underestimating a toxic drug level. Here MSE = 92 / 8 = 11.50, in squared percentage points, a unit nobody can picture. The root mean squared error returns to the units of the outcome:

\[ \text{RMSE} = \sqrt{\text{MSE}} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \hat y_i)^2} . \qquad(3.4)\]

Here RMSE = 3.39 percentage points. The RMSE is never smaller than the MAE, and the gap between them grows when a few errors are much larger than the rest.

The error metrics have one weakness: their size depends on the units and on how variable the outcome is. An MAE of 0.18 mg/dL is good or bad only compared with something. The coefficient of determination (James et al. 2021, ch. 3) makes that comparison explicit. It compares the model’s squared errors with those of the simplest possible prediction, the average outcome \(\bar y\):

\[ R^2 = 1 - \frac{\sum_{i} (y_i - \hat y_i)^2}{\sum_{i} (y_i - \bar y)^2} . \qquad(3.5)\]

An \(R^2\) of 0.6 means the model removes 60 % of the squared error that predicting the average for everyone would make. On new data \(R^2\) can even be negative: the model is then worse than the average. (When \(R^2\) is computed on a test set, \(\bar y\) should be the mean of the training outcomes, the only average available when the model was built.)

Definition 3.2 (Regression metrics) For predictions \(\hat y_i\) of numerical outcomes \(y_i\), the mean absolute error (MAE) is the average of \(\lvert y_i - \hat y_i\rvert\); the mean squared error (MSE) is the average of \((y_i - \hat y_i)^2\); the root mean squared error (RMSE) is the square root of the MSE, in the units of the outcome; and the coefficient of determination \(R^2\) is one minus the ratio of the model’s squared error to the squared error of predicting the mean. Metrics are computed on data the model has not seen.

A close relative of a metric is a loss. The loss \(\mathcal{L}(y, \hat y)\) is the penalty for predicting \(\hat y\) when the truth is \(y\): the squared loss \((y - \hat y)^2\) or the absolute loss \(\lvert y - \hat y \rvert\), for example. Fitting a model means making the average loss on the training data as small as possible; this is the “mark” step of the learning loop of Chapter 1. Evaluating a model means computing a metric on new data. Often the two coincide (least squares fitting minimises the MSE), but they need not: a model can be fitted with squared loss and reported with MAE (Hastie, Tibshirani, and Friedman 2009, ch. 2).

Which metric should you report? Three practical rules, following the v1 notes and the literature:

  • Use the MAE when you want an intuitive average size of error that is not dominated by a few extreme cases (Willmott and Matsuura 2005).
  • Use the RMSE when large errors are disproportionately harmful, and remember that it is sensitive to outliers.
  • Report more than one (for instance MAE and RMSE, plus \(R^2\) against the mean), and always compute them on data the model did not see: on a test set or with cross-validation.

Behind all three rules sits the question of Chapter 1: which decision does the prediction serve? If the team acts when the predicted rise crosses 0.3 mg/dL, an error of 0.1 that pushes a patient across the threshold matters more than an error of 0.3 for a patient far from it. Metrics that weigh errors by their consequences for the decision come in Chapter 5.

3.4 Baseline models: what must a model beat?

The leaflet on Emma’s desk reports an error, but not the error of any alternative. Without a reference point a metric is uninterpretable. The author of this book puts it bluntly to his medical students: an impressive-sounding performance figure reported without a baseline has told you nothing.

Definition 3.3 (Baseline model) A baseline model is the simplest sensible way of making the prediction, against which any proposed model is compared. For a numerical outcome the usual baseline predicts the training mean (or median) for everyone; in clinical work a second baseline is current practice: an existing score, a guideline rule, or a simple model clinicians already trust. A new model earns its place only by beating both by a margin that matters for the decision.

The mean and the median are not arbitrary choices. Among all constant predictions, the mean is the one with the smallest MSE and the median is the one with the smallest MAE (Hastie, Tibshirani, and Friedman 2009, ch. 2). So the “average model” you met in Chapter 1 is the best a model can do without looking at any patient’s measurements: it is the yardstick that measures how much the predictors are worth.

Table 3.3 shows four simple models of Ward 7, each evaluated by five-fold cross-validation on the 1 000 training patients. (The test set stays locked; we open it only in Section 3.8.)

The mean and median give almost identical errors (about 0.24 mg/dL); knowing a patient’s SOFA score reduces the error to 0.196, and adding baseline creatinine to 0.176. Any more elaborate model of these two predictors should now be compared with 0.176, not with the average. Keep this number in mind for Section 3.7.

A model of tumour shrinkage in ONCO-1 has a test-set \(R^2\) of −0.05. What does that mean?

3.5 Cross-validation, step by step

Chapter 1 defined \(V\)-fold cross-validation (Definition 1.9); here is the procedure in full (Stone 1974; James et al. 2021, ch. 5). Take the training set, already divided into \(V\) folds of roughly equal size (on Ward 7, \(V = 5\) folds of 200). Then, for each fold \(v = 1, \dots, V\):

  1. set fold \(v\) aside as the validation fold;
  2. fit the model on the other \(V - 1\) folds, including every data-dependent step (choosing variables, standardising, imputing; see Section 3.6);
  3. predict the outcomes of the validation fold and compute the metric, \(\text{MAE}_v\).

Finally, average the \(V\) results:

\[ \text{CV}_{(V)} = \frac{1}{V}\sum_{v=1}^{V} \text{MAE}_v . \qquad(3.6)\]

Every training patient is used for validation exactly once and for fitting \(V - 1\) times. The spread of the \(V\) fold errors tells you how much the estimate would move with a different split; its standard error, the standard deviation of the fold errors divided by \(\sqrt{V}\), is a rough measure of that uncertainty (Hastie, Tibshirani, and Friedman 2009, ch. 7).

The five fold errors are not identical; they differ by up to a few hundredths of a mg/dL, because some folds happen to contain easier patients than others. That variation is exactly why a single split can mislead, and why averaging over folds gives a steadier estimate.

How many folds? With larger \(V\) each fit uses more of the data, so the estimate is closer to the error of a model trained on all of it, but the fits overlap more, the \(V\) errors are more alike, and the computation grows. Leave-one-out cross-validation (\(V = n\)) is the extreme: almost unbiased, but variable and expensive for slow models. Five or ten folds are the usual compromise (James et al. 2021, ch. 5; Hastie, Tibshirani, and Friedman 2009, ch. 7). Two refinements are common. Repeated cross-validation reruns the whole procedure with several random fold assignments and averages them, which steadies the estimate further. Stratified folds keep the proportion of an outcome (say, AKI yes/no) similar in every fold (Kuhn and Johnson 2013, ch. 4).

Folds must respect the structure of the data. If one patient contributes several rows, as a HomeBeat participant does with one row per day, all of that person’s rows must sit in the same fold; otherwise the model is partly validated on the very people it was fitted on. The same logic, applied to hospitals, gives leave-one-site-out cross-validation: fit on three Ward 7 hospitals, test on the fourth, and rotate. It is the internal-external validation of Section 3.2, and we use it at the end of this chapter.

3.6 Leakage: when the answers slip into the exam

Every honest estimate in this chapter rests on one condition: the model must not have seen, directly or indirectly, the outcomes it is judged on. When that condition fails, the estimate is too good, sometimes absurdly so, and the failure is invisible in the metric itself.

Definition 3.4 (Leakage) Leakage occurs when information that would not be available at the moment of prediction, or information from the data used for evaluation, reaches the model during its development. The result is an estimate of performance that is optimistic, sometimes dramatically, and a model that disappoints when it is used for real (Kaufman et al. 2012; Kapoor and Narayanan 2023).

3.6.1 A cautionary tale from ONCO-1

Recall ONCO-1, the trial in which patients with solid tumours were randomised to chemotherapy or no chemotherapy, and tumour shrinkage was recorded together with the expression of many genes. A natural first question is: which genes are associated with response? Table 3.4 ranks the 50 genes of the browser dataset by their correlation with shrinkage in the 800 training patients.

Three genes stand far above the rest, with correlations of about 0.98: gene_08, gene_14 and gene_19. Fitted into a linear model with the clinical variables, they give a spectacular result (Table 3.5): an \(R^2\) of 0.987 on the test patients. The first version of this book read those three genes as a biological signature of chemotherapy response. That interpretation was wrong, and the reason is instructive.

ONCO-1 is simulated, so we can open the code that generated it (sim_onco1.R). The genes that truly influence shrinkage are gene_01, gene_05 and gene_12. Genes 08, 14 and 19 were built differently: after the outcome had been simulated, their values were overwritten with noisy copies of the shrinkage itself. They do not cause the response; they contain it. In a real study such variables arise more easily than you might think: gene expression measured in tissue removed at surgery, after chemotherapy, already reflects how much the tumour has shrunk; a “baseline” laboratory value recorded days into treatment already reflects the treatment. The tell-tale sign is the same: a predictor that is suspiciously close to the outcome. (ONCO-1 also contains post_tumor_mm, the tumour size after treatment, which is literally an ingredient of the outcome and must never be used to predict it.)

Read the last column. The leaky model looked twice as accurate as the honest one (RMSE 2.10 against 4.40), but in a setting where the three genes are measured before treatment, and so no longer echo the result, its error jumps to 17.20 percentage points, close to the 18.42 of predicting the average for everyone. The honest model is unaffected. The model that wins on paper loses at the bedside. Leakage also distorts the science: in the leaky ranking, the truly causal gene_05 comes fourth, while gene_01 and gene_12, whose effects are small, rank 46th and 47th out of 50. Chapters 4, 6 and 12 return to the question of which genes matter; in this book the three leaky genes stay in the data on purpose, as a standing test of vigilance.

3.6.2 Three ways information leaks

  1. Target leakage: a predictor that would not exist, or would not have this value, at the moment the prediction is needed. The ONCO-1 genes and the 24-hour creatinine in the gate of this chapter are examples.
  2. Train–test contamination: any data-dependent step fitted on rows later used for evaluation. Standardising with the mean of all patients, imputing missing values with the mean of all patients, choosing the genes most correlated with the outcome before splitting, and choosing a hyperparameter with the test set all let the evaluation data shape the model. The cure is to treat every such step as part of the model and refit it inside each training fold (Hastie, Tibshirani, and Friedman 2009, sec. 7.10.2).
  3. Group and time leakage: the same patient, or near-copies of the same record, on both sides of the split; or information from the future used to predict the past.

The second kind deserves a demonstration, because it fools experienced analysts. Take 60 imaginary patients, 2 000 “genes” of pure random noise, and an outcome that is also pure noise: by construction nothing can be predicted. Now build a “gene signature” by selecting the 20 genes most correlated with the outcome and fitting a line on their average. If the 20 genes are selected once, using all 60 patients, and only the line is cross-validated, five-fold cross-validation reports \(R^2\) = 0.67: an apparently strong signature found in noise. If the selection is repeated inside each training fold, as it must be, cross-validation reports \(R^2\) = -0.30: worse than the average, which is the truth. The selection step had already peeked at the validation patients. This mistake was demonstrated on well-known gene-expression datasets of the early 2000s (Ambroise and McLachlan 2002), and a recent survey found leakage of one kind or another in hundreds of published machine-learning studies across many fields (Kapoor and Narayanan 2023).

Figure 3.3 turns the three kinds of leakage into four questions to ask of every feature, every preprocessing step and every split.

Does each practice leak information into the evaluation?

  1. Standardising SOFA and creatinine with the mean and standard deviation of all 1 250 patients, before splitting. Leakage
  2. Using the number of dialysis sessions in the following week as a predictor of AKI. Leakage
  3. Using age, SOFA and lactate on admission to predict the 48-hour creatinine change. No leakage

3.7 \(k\)-nearest neighbours: a first learner

So far our models have been averages and straight lines. Here is a different idea, close to how clinicians reason from experience: to predict what will happen to Aoife, look at the past patients who were most like her, and see what happened to them.

Definition 3.5 (\(k\)-nearest neighbours) \(k\)-nearest neighbours (\(k\)NN) predicts the outcome of a new instance \(\mathbf{x}\) by finding the \(k\) training instances closest to it, the set \(N_k(\mathbf{x})\), and averaging their outcomes (for a numerical outcome) or taking a vote (for a category):

\[ \hat y(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{k}\sum_{i \in N_k(\mathbf{x})} y_i . \qquad(3.7)\]

“Closest” is measured by a distance between feature vectors, usually the Euclidean distance on standardised features (Cover and Hart 1967; James et al. 2021, ch. 2).

\(k\)NN has an unusual property: it has no fitting step. “Training” consists of storing the training patients; all the work happens at prediction time. Two ingredients need care.

Distance. On Ward 7 we use two predictors, the SOFA score and baseline creatinine, and measure how far apart two patients are with the ordinary straight-line (Euclidean) distance in the plane of the two. Chapter 10 is devoted to distances and to what happens to them when there are many predictors; for now the plane is enough.

Scale. SOFA runs from 0 to 18 points; baseline creatinine from about 0.4 to 3 mg/dL. Measured raw, one SOFA point would count as much as a whole mg/dL of creatinine, purely because of the units. So each predictor is standardised: the training mean is subtracted and the result divided by the training standard deviation, putting both on a comparable scale. With standardisation the predictions do not depend on the units. Without it they do: if creatinine is recorded in µmol/L (1 mg/dL = 88.4 µmol/L), its large numbers dominate the distance and the cross-validated MAE of unstandardised \(k\)NN worsens from 0.181 to 0.189 mg/dL, while standardised \(k\)NN gives 0.179 in either unit. Note the word training: the means and standard deviations are learned from the training rows only (from the fitting folds during cross-validation), or the scaling itself leaks (Section 3.6).

The number of neighbours \(k\) controls flexibility. With \(k = 1\) every prediction copies a single past patient: the model follows every accident of the data, the high-variance extreme of Definition 1.8. Its training error is close to zero, because each training patient is (nearly) its own nearest neighbour; on Ward 7 it is 0.072 mg/dL. Its cross-validated error is 0.254, worse than the average model. As \(k\) grows the predictions are averaged over more patients and become smoother; with \(k\) equal to the whole training set every prediction is the training mean, the high-bias extreme. Somewhere in between lies the best compromise, and only data the model has not seen can tell us where.

A flexible learner does not automatically beat a simple one: when the true relationship is close to a straight line, as it is in the simulation behind Ward 7, the line uses all 1 000 patients to estimate two slopes, while \(k\)NN uses only a handful of neighbours for each prediction and pays for that flexibility with variance. Only a fair comparison, on the same folds and against a sensible baseline, reveals this.

Why learn \(k\)NN at all, then? Because it is the simplest learner that has a genuine hyperparameter, because it is easy to explain to a patient (“among 15 past patients most like you, the average rise was…”), and because its weaknesses teach a lot. It needs standardised predictors; it becomes slow when the training set is large, since every prediction scans all of it; and with many predictors “nearest” loses its meaning, a problem Chapter 10 examines.

3.8 Hyperparameters and tuning

In \(y = f(\mathbf{x}) + \varepsilon\), the parameters are the quantities inside \(\hat f\) that the algorithm learns from the data to make the model fit: the intercept and slope of a straight line, for example. The number of neighbours in \(k\)NN is a different kind of quantity. Nothing in the data “fits” it; it decides how the model learns.

Definition 3.6 (Hyperparameter) A hyperparameter is a setting that controls how a model is learned, rather than a quantity learned by the usual fitting step. Hyperparameters set the flexibility of the family of functions the algorithm may choose from, or how aggressively it searches within that family. They are chosen by comparing candidate values on data not used for fitting, usually with cross-validation; this is called tuning.

Ordinary least squares, which fits a straight line by minimising the squared errors, has no hyperparameters: the solution depends only on the data, and its flexibility is fixed by the choice of predictors. Most modern methods have at least one, and each chapter of Part II meets its own:

The standard way to tune is a grid search: list candidate values (here \(k = 1, 2, \dots, 150\)), compute the cross-validated error of each, and choose the best. With several hyperparameters the grid grows quickly, and trying random combinations is often more efficient than a full grid (Bergstra and Bengio 2012). Chapter 4 adds a refinement, the one-standard-error rule, which prefers the simplest model whose error is within one standard error of the best (Hastie, Tibshirani, and Friedman 2009, ch. 7).

3.8.1 The optimism of the minimum, and nested cross-validation

There is a catch. The smallest of 150 cross-validated errors is itself a selected number: part of the reason it is the smallest is that it happened to suit these particular folds. So the minimum CV error is a slightly optimistic estimate of how the tuned model will do (Varma and Simon 2006; Cawley and Talbot 2010). The cure is the logic of Section 3.2 applied once more: the patients that judge the tuning must not take part in it.

Definition 3.7 (Nested cross-validation) In nested cross-validation an outer loop of \(V\) folds estimates performance, and inside each outer training part an inner cross-validation chooses the hyperparameters. Each outer fold is predicted by a model whose hyperparameters were chosen without it, so the average outer error is an honest estimate of the performance of the whole procedure, tuning included (Varma and Simon 2006).

On Ward 7, nested cross-validation chose \(k\) = 28, 19, 22, 13, 22 in the five outer folds (different, because each inner search saw slightly different patients) and gave an average outer MAE of 0.181 mg/dL, against the minimum CV error of 0.179. The optimism is tiny here, because there is one hyperparameter, a flat error curve and 1 000 patients. It becomes large when there are few patients, many predictors or many hyperparameters, which is exactly the situation of many omics and imaging studies (Varma and Simon 2006).

3.8.2 Opening the test set

All choices are now made: \(k\)NN with \(k\) = 15, standardised SOFA and baseline creatinine. The model is refitted on all 1 000 training patients and, for the first and only time, applied to the 250 test patients. Its test MAE is 0.176 mg/dL, in line with the cross-validated estimate (0.179). For the record, the two-predictor straight line scores 0.171 and the average model 0.236 on the same patients: the ranking found by cross-validation holds. The agreement between cross-validated and test errors is what an honest workflow should produce; a large gap would be a warning that something in the process leaked or that the test patients differ from the training ones.

Two predictors were a teaching choice. 3.12 Summary

  • A trustworthy workflow splits the data: fit on a training set, make choices with cross-validation (or a validation set), and open a locked test set once. Ward 7 and ONCO-1 have fixed, book-wide splits.
  • Metrics summarise errors \(e_i = y_i - \hat y_i\): MAE (typical miss), MSE and RMSE (big misses weighted up), and \(R^2\) (improvement over predicting the mean). Choose the metric that matches what an error costs.
  • Every model must beat a baseline: the mean (or median) and current practice.
  • \(V\)-fold cross-validation fits \(V\) times and averages the held-out errors; folds must keep each patient (or site) together.
  • Leakage (features that contain the outcome, steps fitted on evaluation rows, or the same patient on both sides) makes models look better than they are. ONCO-1’s genes 08, 14 and 19 are the book’s standing example.
  • \(k\)-nearest neighbours averages the outcomes of the \(k\) most similar training patients; it needs standardised predictors, and \(k\) trades variance (small \(k\)) for bias (large \(k\)).
  • A hyperparameter such as \(k\) is tuned by cross-validation; nested cross-validation estimates the performance of the whole tuning procedure. Then the test set is opened, once.
