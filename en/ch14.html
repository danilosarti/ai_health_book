How to use this chapter

Starting here?

This chapter borrows a few ideas from earlier chapters. In brief:

  • Decisions (Definition 1.4, Section 1.5). A decision chooses one alternative \(a\) from a set \(\mathcal{A}\) using what is known, \(\mathcal{K}\). With probabilities and values, the best alternative is the one with the highest expected value — the probability-weighted average of the scores of its consequences.
  • Expectation and the Bernoulli distribution (Chapter 2). \(\mathbb{E}[Y]\) is the long-run average of \(Y\); a yes/no outcome with probability \(p\) of “yes” has expectation \(p\). Information has value only if it can change a decision (value of information).
  • The beta posterior (Chapter 8). Starting from a \(\text{Beta}(1, 1)\) (flat) prior for a proportion and observing \(k\) successes in \(n\) trials gives a \(\text{Beta}(1 + k,\, 1 + n - k)\) posterior.
  • Observational data and confounding (Definition 1.2, Section 1.10, Chapter 13). In routine care, treatment follows the patient’s condition, so comparing treated and untreated patients mixes the effect of treatment with the reasons for it. Causal estimates need exchangeability (no unmeasured confounding) and positivity (every kind of patient had a chance of each treatment).
  • Learning loop (Section 1.6). Guess, mark how wrong the guess is, adjust, repeat. Gradient descent in Chapter 7 uses a learning rate \(\eta\) to set the size of each adjustment.

14.1 Decisions that come back

Every decision in this book so far has been taken once. Emma decided on Monday night whether to start the kidney-protective bundle for Aoife; a model supplied a probability, the values supplied a threshold, and the decision was made (Section 1.5). Real care rarely stops there. On Tuesday morning Aoife’s creatinine is measured again and the team decides about fluids; the fluids change her blood pressure and her kidneys; on Wednesday the next creatinine reflects both her illness and Tuesday’s choice. Giving too little fluid risks under-perfusing the kidneys, giving too much risks fluid overload, and the right amount today depends on what was given yesterday. The insulin infusion works the same way, every two hours instead of every day.

Three features make these problems different from the prediction problems of Chapters 3–9:

  • Actions change the data. The glucose reading at 10 am depends on the rate chosen at 8 am. A model of “glucose next” that ignores the action is a model of the past policy, not of the patient.
  • Consequences are delayed. A large insulin increase may look good for two hours and cause hypoglycaemia four hours later. Credit and blame must be spread over a sequence of actions.
  • Nobody labels the right action. There is no column “correct rate” in the records, only what was done and what followed. The learner must discover good actions from their consequences.

Reinforcement learning (RL) is the branch of machine learning built for exactly this setting: an agent learns, from interaction and reward, a rule for acting that does well over time (Sutton and Barto 2018). Chapter 1 met it in one paragraph (Section 1.6.2); this chapter opens it up.

Definition 14.1 (Agent, environment, state, action, reward) An agent is whatever chooses the actions: a clinician, a protocol, an algorithm. The environment is everything the agent acts on and cannot fully control — here, the patient. At each decision time \(t\) the agent observes the state \(s_t\), a summary of what is known that matters for what happens next; it takes an action \(a_t\) from a set \(\mathcal{A}\); the environment responds by moving to a new state \(s_{t+1}\) and returning a reward \(r_{t+1}\), a number that scores how good that step was. The cycle then repeats (Sutton and Barto 2018, ch. 3).

The action set is Chapter 1’s set of alternatives, \(\mathcal{A}\), and the action \(a_t\) is the chosen alternative \(a\) — now chosen once per time step instead of once in total. Figure 14.1 draws the cycle for Aoife’s infusion.

Definition 14.2 (Policy) A policy is a rule for choosing actions from states. A deterministic policy names one action for each state; a stochastic policy gives a probability \(\pi(a \mid s)\) to each action \(a\) in state \(s\). A bedside protocol (“above 10 mmol/L increase, below 6 decrease, otherwise keep”) is a deterministic policy written by people; reinforcement learning learns a policy from rewards (Sutton and Barto 2018, ch. 3).

The symbol \(\pi\) meant a prevalence in Chapter 2; in this chapter, as everywhere in the reinforcement-learning literature, it means a policy. The reward also needs a word of warning before we use it. It is a design choice, not a fact about the patient: someone decides that a glucose in range earns \(+1\) and hypoglycaemia \(-10\). The agent will pursue exactly what the reward says, which may not be what we meant. We return to this in Section 14.5; keep it in mind as you read.

The Ward 7 kidney decisions fit the same frame. The state on Tuesday morning would include creatinine, urine output, fluid balance and blood pressure; the actions are fluids, a diuretic or nothing; a reward might penalise each rise in creatinine of 0.3 mg/dL or more and each litre of excess fluid. That problem is much harder to stylise honestly, which is why the worked example of this chapter is the simpler glucose problem.

A team has two years of Ward 7 records of insulin rates and glucose readings. Why is learning a dosing policy from them not an ordinary supervised-learning problem?

14.2 Learning while choosing: multi-armed bandits

Start with the simplest sequential problem, one where the action does not change the state. In the HomeBeat digital trial, 500 people wear a smartwatch at home; Lena is one of them. When a participant’s stress score is high in the evening, the app sends one short nudge. The design team has three candidates — a breathing exercise, a short walk, and a sleep tip — and records a reward of 1 if the participant does the suggested activity within an hour, 0 if not. Nobody knows which nudge works best. Every evening the app must choose one, for one person, and learn from the result.

Definition 14.3 (Multi-armed bandit) A multi-armed bandit is a repeated choice among \(K\) actions (arms, after a row of slot machines), each with an unknown reward distribution of mean \(\mu_k\). At each round the agent picks one arm, observes its reward and nothing about the others, and aims to collect as much reward as possible over many rounds (Robbins 1952; Lattimore and Szepesvári 2020). There is no state that carries over: each round is a fresh participant, and only the agent’s knowledge changes.

The natural estimate of an arm’s mean reward is the average of the rewards it has produced so far, \(\hat\mu_k\). It need not be recomputed from scratch: after the \(n\)-th reward \(r\) from arm \(k\),

\[ \hat\mu_k \leftarrow \hat\mu_k + \frac{1}{n}\,\bigl(r - \hat\mu_k\bigr), \qquad(14.1)\]

which says: move the old estimate a fraction of the way towards the new observation. The difference \(r - \hat\mu_k\) is a prediction error; the fraction \(1/n\) shrinks as evidence accumulates. Keep the shape new estimate = old estimate + step × error in mind: Q-learning in Section 14.4 uses it too.

14.2.1 Exploration versus exploitation

Suppose that after ten evenings the breathing exercise has worked 3 times out of 4, the short walk 1 time out of 3 and the sleep tip 1 time out of 3. A greedy agent always picks the arm with the best estimate — the breathing exercise. But four evenings is very little evidence. If the walk is in fact the best nudge, the greedy agent may never find out, because it will never try the walk again often enough for its estimate to recover from an unlucky start.

Definition 14.4 (Exploration and exploitation) Exploiting means choosing the action that looks best given what is known now. Exploring means choosing an action that looks worse, or is uncertain, in order to learn about it. Every agent that learns while it acts must balance the two: exploit too much and it may settle on the wrong action for ever; explore too much and it keeps giving people actions it already knows to be inferior (Sutton and Barto 2018, ch. 2).

Exploration is the value of information of Chapter 2 in a new guise: trying the walk again costs a little now (a nudge that is probably slightly worse) and buys information that may change every future decision. Chapter 8 made the same point for one patient: when a posterior straddles a decision threshold, more information is worth seeking. Two simple strategies put this into practice.

  • \(\epsilon\)-greedy. With probability \(1 - \epsilon\) exploit (pick the arm with the highest \(\hat\mu_k\)); with probability \(\epsilon\) pick an arm at random. With \(\epsilon = 0.1\), one evening in ten is spent exploring, for ever. The rule is crude — it explores the obviously bad arm as often as the promising one — but robust and easy to explain. (The \(\epsilon\) here has nothing to do with the error term \(\varepsilon\) of Chapter 1; the name is too well established to change.)
  • Thompson sampling. Keep a posterior for each arm’s success probability — with a flat prior and \(k\) successes in \(n\) trials, a \(\text{Beta}(1 + k, 1 + n - k)\) distribution, exactly as in Chapter 8. Each evening draw one random value from each arm’s posterior and pick the arm with the largest draw. An arm is therefore chosen with the probability that it is the best, given the evidence: uncertain arms get explored, clearly inferior ones fade away, and exploration dies down by itself as evidence accumulates (Thompson 1933; Russo et al. 2018).

Thompson’s paper of 1933, the origin of the method, was about clinical trials: how to allocate patients between two treatments so that fewer receive the worse one while the evidence accumulates (Thompson 1933).

14.2.2 Bandits and adaptive trials

The bandit is the mathematical skeleton of a response-adaptive clinical trial, in which the allocation probabilities shift during the trial towards the arm that is doing better (Villar, Bowden, and Wason 2015; Pallmann et al. 2018). The appeal is ethical: fewer patients receive the inferior treatment. The costs are statistical and practical. A trial must also estimate the difference between arms with known error rates, and bandit allocation, which starves the weaker arm of patients, can reduce power and bias the estimate; if the patients enrolled late differ from those enrolled early (a time trend), adaptive allocation can mislead (Thall, Fox, and Wathen 2015). Equal allocation is the best design for learning; bandits are designed for earning while learning. Which matters more is a question of values, not of mathematics.

The same tension appears in HomeBeat. A bandit that quickly settles on the walk serves participants well, but tells the team little about whether the sleep tip might suit some people better. Real mobile-health programmes therefore use contextual bandits, in which the choice depends on features of the person and the moment (time of day, recent activity, stress), and test them in micro-randomised trials that randomise each decision point (Klasnja et al. 2015; Liao et al. 2020). Lena’s best nudge at 9 pm after a sedentary day may differ from her best nudge on a Sunday morning.

A trial of two antibiotics uses Thompson sampling to allocate patients, so that the arm with the better cure rate so far receives more patients. Compared with fixed 1:1 randomisation, what is given up?

14.3 Planning ahead: Markov decision processes

In a bandit, today’s choice does not change tomorrow’s situation. On Aoife’s infusion it does: raising the rate now lowers the glucose the next nurse will find. To plan, we need a model of how states follow one another.

Definition 14.5 (Markov decision process (MDP)) A Markov decision process consists of a set of states, a set of actions, transition probabilities \(P(s' \mid s, a)\) — the chance that the next state is \(s'\) when action \(a\) is taken in state \(s\) — and a reward for each transition. It is Markov because the next state depends only on the current state and action, not on how the patient got there: the state must summarise everything about the past that matters for the future (Bellman 1957; Sutton and Barto 2018, ch. 3).

The Markov property is a demand on the state, and it is where clinical realism usually breaks first. If Aoife’s response to insulin depends on whether she was fed in the last four hours, then a state that contains only her current glucose is not Markov; the state must include feeding too.

14.3.1 The toy insulin problem

Here is the problem we will solve, learn and break for the rest of the chapter.

  • States. Glucose in one of five bands — below 4 mmol/L (hypoglycaemia), 4–6, 6–10 (the target range), 10–14, above 14 — combined with the current infusion rate: off, low, medium or high. That makes \(5 \times 4 = 20\) states. (10 mmol/L is about 180 mg/dL.)
  • Actions. Every two hours: decrease the rate by one level, keep it, or increase it by one level (you cannot go below off or above high).
  • Transitions. Over the next two hours, glucose moves down one band, stays, or moves up one band, with probabilities that depend on the new rate (Table 14.2). Without insulin, the stress of sepsis pushes glucose up; at the high rate it tends to fall.
  • Rewards. Ending a two-hour period in range earns \(+1\); above 14 mmol/L, \(-1\); below 4 mmol/L, \(-10\), because hypoglycaemia is dangerous; the other bands earn 0.

The intensive-care trial NICE-SUGAR is a reminder that targets are themselves decisions: aiming for glucose of 4.5–6.0 mmol/L instead of 10 mmol/L or less caused more severe hypoglycaemia and higher mortality (NICE-SUGAR Study Investigators 2009). Our target band of 6–10 mmol/L reflects that lesson.

14.3.2 Returns and discounting

A policy produces a sequence of rewards, and we need one number that says how good the sequence is.

Definition 14.6 (Return and discount factor) The return from time \(t\) is the discounted sum of the rewards that follow,

\[ G_t = r_{t+1} + \gamma\, r_{t+2} + \gamma^2 r_{t+3} + \dots = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \gamma^k\, r_{t+k+1}, \qquad(14.2)\]

where the discount factor \(\gamma\), between 0 and 1, sets how much a reward \(k\) steps away counts compared with an immediate one (Sutton and Barto 2018, ch. 3).

With \(\gamma = 0\) only the next reward matters: a perfectly short-sighted agent. As \(\gamma\) approaches 1 the agent cares about the distant future almost as much as about the next two hours. Discounting also keeps the infinite sum finite. For example, if Aoife’s next five periods earn rewards \(0, 0, 1, 1, 1\) (two more periods above range, then three in range) and we stop there, the return with \(\gamma = 0.9\) is \(0 + 0.9 \times 0 + 0.81 \times 1 + 0.729 \times 1 + 0.6561 \times 1 =\) 2.20. In this chapter \(\gamma = 0.9\): a reward ten steps (20 hours) away counts about a third as much as an immediate one.

14.3.3 Value functions and the Bellman equation

Definition 14.7 (Value functions) The value of a state under policy \(\pi\), \(V^\pi(s)\), is the expected return when starting in \(s\) and following \(\pi\). The action value \(Q^\pi(s, a)\) is the expected return when starting in \(s\), taking action \(a\) once, and following \(\pi\) afterwards. The best achievable values are written \(V^*(s)\) and \(Q^*(s, a)\); a policy that picks, in every state, an action with the highest \(Q^*(s,a)\) is an optimal policy (Sutton and Barto 2018, ch. 3).

(The letter \(V\) also counts cross-validation folds in Chapter 3; with its argument \((s)\) and its superscript there is no risk of confusion here.)

Values obey a simple recursion, first written by Richard Bellman (Bellman 1957): the value of where you are equals the expected reward of the next step plus the discounted value of where you land. For a fixed policy,

\[ V^\pi(s) = \sum_{a} \pi(a \mid s) \sum_{s'} P(s' \mid s, a)\,\bigl[\, r(s') + \gamma\, V^\pi(s') \,\bigr], \qquad(14.3)\]

and for the best policy, which takes the best action instead of averaging over \(\pi\),

\[ V^*(s) = \max_{a} \sum_{s'} P(s' \mid s, a)\,\bigl[\, r(s') + \gamma\, V^*(s') \,\bigr] = \max_a Q^*(s, a). \qquad(14.4)\]

Here \(r(s')\) is the reward for landing in \(s'\). The Bellman equation turns one hard question (what is the value of a whole future?) into many easy ones (what is one step worth, given the values of the next states?). Value iteration exploits this: start with all values at zero and apply Equation 14.4 to every state, again and again, until the values stop changing (Sutton and Barto 2018, ch. 4). Each sweep looks one step further into the future, and with \(\gamma < 1\) the values converge to \(V^*\).

Run on the toy insulin problem, value iteration gives the optimal policy in Table 14.3. One Bellman backup shows where its numbers come from. Take the state “glucose 10–14, infusion low” and the action increase: the rate becomes medium, so glucose falls to 6–10 with probability 0.30 (reward \(+1\)), stays at 10–14 with probability 0.60 (reward 0) or rises above 14 with probability 0.10 (reward \(-1\)). With the optimal values of the three landing states, 4.49, 4.33 and 3.03,

\[ Q^*(\text{10–14 low}, \text{increase}) = 0.30\,(1 + 0.9 \times 4.49) + 0.60\,(0 + 0.9 \times 4.33) + 0.10\,(-1 + 0.9 \times 3.03) = 4.03. \]

The same calculation for keep gives 3.02 and for decrease 1.28, so increasing is the best action in that state.

The policy is sensible but not obvious. Below 6 mmol/L it always lowers the rate (or leaves the infusion off). Above 10 it raises it, except at the high rate, where it holds. Inside the target range it does something a simple protocol would not: with the infusion off it starts it (because glucose will otherwise drift up), and at the medium or high rate it lowers it before glucose leaves the range (because glucose will otherwise keep falling). It anticipates. Compared with a bedside “sliding scale” (increase above 10, decrease below 6, otherwise keep), starting from glucose 10–14 with the infusion off, the optimal policy keeps glucose in range for 46 % of the next 24 hours against 34 %, and in hypoglycaemia for 0.7 % against 5.5 % — in this toy, whose transition probabilities we invented and therefore know exactly.

14.4 Learning from experience: Q-learning

Value iteration needs the transition probabilities of Table 14.2. For a real patient nobody has them. Can an agent find a good policy just by acting and watching what happens? This is what model-free reinforcement learning does, and Q-learning is its classic algorithm (Watkins and Dayan 1992).

Definition 14.8 (Q-learning) Q-learning keeps a table of estimates \(\hat Q(s, a)\), one for every state and action, all starting at zero. After each step — state \(s\), action \(a\), reward \(r\), next state \(s'\) — it moves the estimate towards a better-informed target:

\[ \hat Q(s, a) \leftarrow \hat Q(s, a) + \eta\,\Bigl[\, \underbrace{r + \gamma \max_{a'} \hat Q(s', a')}_{\text{target}} - \hat Q(s, a) \,\Bigr], \qquad(14.5)\]

where \(\eta\) is the step size. Actions are usually chosen \(\epsilon\)-greedily from the current table. If every state–action pair keeps being tried and the step size shrinks suitably, the estimates converge to \(Q^*\) (Watkins and Dayan 1992; Sutton and Barto 2018, ch. 6).

Look at the shape: new estimate = old estimate + step × (target − old estimate), as in Equation 14.1. The target is a one-step version of the Bellman optimality equation: the reward actually received, plus the discounted value of the best action in the state actually reached, as currently estimated. No transition probabilities appear; the environment supplies samples of them, one step at a time. The \(\max\) in the target is what makes Q-learning off-policy: it learns the value of acting greedily even while it is exploring, so the exploratory steps do not spoil what it learns.

A worked step. Suppose the agent is in “10–14, low”, with current estimates \(-0.4\), \(0.8\) and \(1.6\) for decrease, keep and increase. It increases the rate; glucose falls to 6–10 (reward \(+1\)) and the next state is “6–10, medium”, whose estimates are \(2.1\), \(2.9\) and \(1.2\). With \(\eta = 0.1\) and \(\gamma = 0.9\) the target is \(1 + 0.9 \times 2.9 = 3.61\), the error is \(3.61 - 1.6 = 2.01\), and the estimate moves to \(1.6 + 0.1 \times 2.01 = 1.801\). 14.5 Reinforcement learning in health care: regimes, records and rewards

The toy problem made three assumptions that clinical reality breaks: the agent could experiment freely, the state captured everything that mattered, and the reward was what we wanted. This section takes each in turn.

14.5.1 Dynamic treatment regimes

Long before the term reinforcement learning reached medicine, statisticians had formalised sequential treatment decisions as dynamic treatment regimes (Murphy 2003; Chakraborty and Murphy 2014).

Definition 14.9 (Dynamic treatment regime) A dynamic treatment regime is a sequence of decision rules, one for each decision point, each mapping the information available about a patient at that time — the history of measurements and past treatments — to a recommended treatment. A regime is dynamic because the treatment adapts to how the patient evolves; an optimal regime maximises the expected value of a chosen final outcome (Murphy 2003).

A dynamic treatment regime is a policy in the language of this chapter, written for a few decision points (often two or three) with a patient’s whole history as the state. Figure 14.2 shows a two-stage regime for Ward 7 built on the kidney decisions of Chapters 1, 5 and 13.

Optimal regimes can be estimated by a statistical cousin of Q-learning that works backwards: first model the outcome as a function of the stage-2 history and treatment, and choose the best stage-2 action for each history; then model the outcome that each patient would reach if treated optimally at stage 2 as a function of the stage-1 history and treatment (Murphy 2003; Chakraborty and Murphy 2014). The best data for this are not records but sequential multiple assignment randomised trials (SMARTs), which randomise patients again at each decision point, so that every stage’s treatment comparison is protected by randomisation (Murphy 2005). A SMART is to a dynamic regime what ONCO-1 is to a single treatment decision.

14.5.2 Offline reinforcement learning: learning from records

Nobody may experiment on Aoife to train an agent. The obvious alternative is to learn from records: millions of past decisions and their consequences. This is offline (or batch) reinforcement learning: learning a policy from a fixed set of logged transitions, collected under someone else’s policy, with no further interaction (Levine et al. 2020). It is attractive, and it inherits every problem of Chapter 13, plus one of its own.

  • Confounding. In the records, actions were chosen by clinicians who saw the patient. If sicker patients received more insulin, more fluid or more vasopressor, the data will associate those treatments with worse outcomes, and a naive learner will conclude that they are harmful — the Ward 7 adherence problem of Chapters 1 and 13, now repeated at every time step. Any variable that influenced the clinicians and is missing from the state (how the patient looked, a family’s wishes) is an unmeasured confounder that no algorithm can adjust for (Gottesman et al. 2019).
  • Positivity. An agent can only learn the consequences of actions that were actually taken in similar states. Where clinicians never did something, the data say nothing about it, and the learner’s estimates there are pure extrapolation.

The toy problem shows the second danger starkly. Suppose the records come from nurses who always follow the sliding scale. Replaying those logged transitions through the Q-learning update (the Lab does it), the agent never sees 68 % of the state–action pairs: their estimates stay at zero, the value they started with. In the states where every action that was tried led to bad outcomes (near hypoglycaemia, for example), an untried action with an estimate of zero looks better than all of them. The learned policy chooses it — and, simulated on the true toy patient, it spends 28 % of the next 24 hours in hypoglycaemia, against 5.5 % for the nurses it learned from. The records were perfectly accurate; the problem is that they never contained the information the agent needed. Offline methods counter this by penalising actions far from those in the data, at the cost of staying close to current practice (Levine et al. 2020).

The most cited example in critical care is the AI Clinician (Komorowski et al. 2018). Using records of tens of thousands of ICU patients with sepsis from two large US databases, it summarised each patient’s condition every four hours, from 24 hours before to 48 hours after the estimated onset of sepsis, in one of 750 states (found by clustering, as in Chapter 11), plus two terminal states for death and discharge. It chose among 25 actions — five dose levels of intravenous fluids combined with five of vasopressors — and received a reward only at the end of each patient’s record: \(+100\) if the patient survived to 90 days, \(-100\) if not. In retrospective analyses, the value estimated for its policy exceeded that of the clinicians’, and patients whose actual doses happened to be close to its recommendations had the lowest mortality. It was a research study; it was never used to treat patients.

The study triggered a useful debate. Critics argued that the retrospective evaluation gave little weight to the patients whose treatment differed from the policy, so that the estimates of its value rested on far fewer effective patients than the size of the databases suggested, and that a reward based only on 90-day survival ignored short-term goals such as blood pressure (Jeter et al. 2019); the authors replied, defending their methods and sensitivity analyses (Komorowski et al. 2019). Shortly afterwards, several of the same researchers and others published guidelines that boil down to three questions for any reinforcement learning study from health records (Gottesman et al. 2019):

  1. Did the agent see all the variables that influenced the clinicians’ decisions? If not, confounding can make any policy look good or bad.
  2. How much data supported the evaluation, really? Estimating a new policy’s value from records (off-policy evaluation) reweights the patients whose treatment happened to agree with it (Precup, Sutton, and Singh 2000); when few agree, the estimate rests on a handful of patients however large the database.
  3. Will the policy behave as intended prospectively? Does it recommend actions clinicians never take, does it make clinical sense, and what happens when it is wrong?

With the clinically weighted reward the agent keeps hypoglycaemia to 0.7 % of the day. Rewarding only time in range gives the same policy here, because in this toy leaving the range downwards is already costly enough. But “+1 whenever glucose is below 10 mmol/L” — a reward that sounds like “control hyperglycaemia” — produces a policy that turns the infusion up and keeps it up: hypoglycaemia for 48 % of the day, and time in range down to 15 %. The agent did exactly what it was asked. Nothing in its reward said that 3 mmol/L is worse than 9.

The same lesson runs through real cases. The AI Clinician’s reward ignored everything that happened between admission and day 90, including harms from the treatment itself. Language models are tuned with reinforcement learning from human ratings, and learn to produce answers that raters prefer, which is not always the same as answers that are true (Ouyang et al. 2022); Chapter 18 returns to this.

14.5.4 Evaluating and deploying a learned policy safely

Putting the pieces together, a clinical team facing a reinforcement-learning product — Emma and Seán with their brochure — can ask:

  • Which reward, and who chose it? Is it the outcome patients care about, or a convenient proxy? Are harms (hypoglycaemia, fluid overload, side effects) in it explicitly?
  • What was the state, and what was missing from it? Were the variables that drove clinicians’ decisions recorded? If not, how was confounding handled?
  • How was the policy evaluated? By off-policy estimates from records (how many effective patients?), in a simulator (how was it validated?), or prospectively? Does it recommend actions outside the range of current practice?
  • What constrains it? Hard limits on doses and rates of change; recommendations shown to a clinician rather than executed automatically; a way to override and to log overrides.
  • How will it be monitored? As practice changes, the data the policy sees drift away from the data it learned from (Chapter 3); performance and safety events need watching.

In the European Union, software that recommends doses for individual patients is a medical device (European Parliament and Council of the European Union 2017; Medical Device Coordination Group 2025), and AI systems used as safety components of medical devices are classed as high-risk, with obligations for risk management, data governance, human oversight and post-market monitoring (European Parliament and Council of the European Union 2024). A learned policy reaches the bedside, if at all, the way any new treatment strategy does: through prospective evaluation, ideally randomised.

14.6 Where this fits in the decision loop

Every earlier chapter drew the decision loop once per decision. Reinforcement learning is the decision loop run repeatedly, with the action–outcome arrow feeding straight back into the next decision and into the learning itself. That changes where things can go wrong. The values of Chapter 1 no longer enter only at the threshold: they are written into the reward, once, by someone, and then optimised relentlessly; a reward that misses a harm builds that blind spot into every future decision. The information link depends on causal knowledge: what each action would do in each state, which records alone supply only under strong assumptions (Chapter 13). And the human in the loop has a clear role: to own the reward, to check that recommendations make clinical sense, and to keep the authority to override. So what should Emma and Seán reply to the brochure? Show us the reward, the variables the agent saw, how its policy was evaluated and on how many effective patients, and the constraints it works within; then let us test it prospectively, with clinicians deciding.

Selected answers

1. The agent is the app’s selection algorithm; the environment is the participant (and their evening); the actions are the three nudges; the reward is 1 if the participant does the activity within an hour. No state carries over: each round is treated as independent. It would become an MDP if, for example, today’s nudge changed tomorrow’s responsiveness (fatigue with repeated nudges), so that the history of nudges had to be part of the state.

3. With equal rates every strategy has zero regret, because no nudge is better than another. The bandit strategies still concentrate on whichever nudge had a lucky start, so a statistician would get unbalanced groups and a less precise comparison; equal allocation gives the most information that the nudges really are equivalent.

4. For \(\gamma = 0.5\): \(1 + 0.5 + 0 + 0 - 10 \times 0.0625 = 0.875\). For \(\gamma = 0.9\): \(1 + 0.9 + 0 + 0 - 10 \times 0.6561 = -4.661\). The far-sighted agent (\(\gamma = 0.9\)) is much more troubled by the hypoglycaemia four steps ahead.

5. At the medium rate glucose moves down with probability 0.30 and up with only 0.10 per step: left alone it tends to fall, eventually towards hypoglycaemia, which costs −10. Lowering the rate while still in range trades a small risk of drifting above 10 for a lower risk of a heavily penalised fall. The sliding scale reacts only after glucose has left the range.

7. Random deviations cover many more state–action pairs (positivity improves): in our run every pair was tried at least once, and the offline agent’s policy came close to the optimal one, with less hypoglycaemia than the nurses it learned from. This is why exploration, or randomisation in a SMART, makes data far more useful for learning policies — and why, without it, offline methods must stay close to the actions in the data.

14.9 Summary

  • Reinforcement learning learns a policy — a rule from states to actions — from the rewards that follow the actions. It suits decisions that recur, whose consequences are delayed, and where the actions change the data.
  • A multi-armed bandit is the simplest case: repeated choices with no state that carries over. The agent must balance exploration and exploitation; ε-greedy explores at random, Thompson sampling explores in proportion to the posterior probability that an arm is best. Bandits underlie response-adaptive trials, which trade some statistical information for better outcomes during the trial.
  • A Markov decision process adds states and transition probabilities. The return is the discounted sum of future rewards; value functions \(V^\pi\) and \(Q^\pi\) give its expectation; the Bellman equation writes each value as one step’s reward plus the discounted value of the next state. Value iteration solves known MDPs.
  • Q-learning estimates \(Q^*\) from experience alone with the update old + η × (target − old); it learns the greedy policy’s value while exploring. It needs every action to be tried.
  • In health care, sequential policies are dynamic treatment regimes, ideally learned from SMARTs. Offline RL from records inherits confounding and positivity problems, and off-policy evaluation can rest on few effective patients.
  • The reward encodes values. A proxy reward invites reward hacking; harms must be in the reward or excluded by constraints, and a learned policy needs prospective evaluation and human oversight.
