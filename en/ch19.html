A request HomeBeat cannot refuse, and cannot grant

The HomeBeat digital trial follows 500 people who wear a smartwatch at home; Lena, 72, is one of them. This morning the trial team received an email from a university group that builds early-warning algorithms for wearables: “Could we have your data? We only need the numbers, not the names.” The trial’s data protection officer says no. Removing names does not make 500 detailed physiological profiles anonymous, and the participants consented to one trial, not to every algorithm in the country.

The trial statistician proposes a third way: “Let’s send them synthetic participants: records that look like ours, behave like ours, and belong to nobody.” Across town, Dr Emma Walsh, the intensivist who chaired her hospital’s review of a chest X-ray tool in Chapter 7, hears the same word from a vendor: synthetic X-rays to enlarge a small training set. Can a machine make convincing data that belong to nobody? How would anyone check that they are convincing, useful and safe? By the end of this chapter you will have generated synthetic X-rays and synthetic HomeBeat participants, and audited both.

19.1 Two questions a model can answer

Every model you have met since Chapter 3 answers one kind of question: given what I see about this patient, what is the outcome? Logistic regression gives Priya’s probability of acute kidney injury; a convolutional network gives the probability that an X-ray shows pneumonia. In the language of Chapter 2, these models estimate a conditional distribution, \(P(y \mid \mathbf{x})\): the outcome, given the features. They draw a boundary between outcomes, and they are called discriminative.

A second kind of model asks a different question: what do the data themselves look like? It learns the distribution of the features, \(p(\mathbf{x})\) (or of features and outcome together, \(p(\mathbf{x}, y)\)) well enough to draw new examples from it. Chapter 1 called this generative modelling (Section 1.6.2) and placed generative AI beside predictive AI (Section 1.6).

Definition 19.1 (Generative and discriminative models) A discriminative model learns the distribution of an outcome given the features, \(P(y \mid \mathbf{x})\), and is used to predict or classify. A generative model learns the distribution of the data, \(p(\mathbf{x})\) or \(p(\mathbf{x}, y)\), and can therefore sample from it: produce new examples that resemble the training data without copying them.

The two are linked by Bayes’ theorem. A generative model of \(p(\mathbf{x} \mid y)\) and \(P(y)\) can be turned into a classifier, \(P(y \mid \mathbf{x}) \propto p(\mathbf{x} \mid y)\,P(y)\); the reverse is not true, because a discriminative model never learns what \(\mathbf{x}\) looks like. Learning the whole distribution is a harder task than drawing a boundary: with plenty of data, discriminative classifiers usually reach lower error, while generative ones can do better when data are scarce (Ng and Jordan 2001). You have already met generative models in this book without the name. The Gaussian mixture of Chapter 11 describes each cluster of patients as a cloud from which new patients could be drawn. Multiple imputation in Chapter 9 generates plausible values for Lena’s missing nights: the trap in the gate.

And there is a larger one. Every running example in this book is synthetic. The 1 250 Ward 7 patients, the ONCO-1 trial, HomeBeat and CellWorks were all produced by short scripts that the author wrote by hand (listed in the data appendix): draw an age, then a severity that depends on age, then a creatinine change that depends on both, plus noise. Each script is a generative model, written from knowledge instead of learned from data. This chapter is about learning such models from data, when the data are too rich (784 pixels of an X-ray, nine interlocking signals of a smartwatch, the atoms of a molecule) to write the recipe by hand.

The simplest learned generative model is one you can build in a line. Chapter 2 described resting heart rate in HomeBeat with a normal distribution; estimate its mean and standard deviation, and rnorm() produces as many new heart rates as you like. The difficulty starts when several variables must be generated together: a frail, inflamed 75-year-old should get a high heart rate and a low heart-rate variability and a low activity index. For an X-ray the “variables” are 784 pixels whose values depend on each other in intricate ways: ribs are continuous, lungs are symmetric, the heart sits on the left. The rest of the chapter shows four ways of learning such joint distributions, three with neural networks and one with plain statistics.

Which of these models is generative?

19.2 Latent variables and autoencoders

Look at how HomeBeat was simulated. Each participant first receives an age, a frailty and a level of baseline inflammation; the smartwatch signals (resting heart rate, heart-rate variability, skin temperature, activity) are then generated from those few hidden quantities, plus noise (sim_homebeat.R). If frailty and inflammation had not been recorded, they would still be there, shaping every signal from behind the scenes. Such hidden causes are called latent variables.

Definition 19.2 (Latent variable) A latent variable is a quantity that is not observed directly but helps to explain, or to generate, the variables that are. A latent-variable model generates an example in two steps: draw a short code \(\mathbf{z}\) from a simple distribution, then turn \(\mathbf{z}\) into an example \(\mathbf{x}\).

Clinicians reason with latent variables all the time: “frailty”, “physiological reserve”, “disease activity” are not measured by any single number, but they summarise many. The cluster membership of Chapter 11 is a latent variable too. For an X-ray, nobody knows what the right latent variables are. The idea of the methods below is to let a neural network discover them.

19.2.1 The autoencoder

An autoencoder is a neural network trained to copy its input to its output through a narrow passage (Hinton and Salakhutdinov 2006). The first half, the encoder, squeezes the 784 pixels of an X-ray into a few numbers \(\mathbf{z}\), in this chapter just two. The second half, the decoder, tries to rebuild the 784 pixels from those two numbers. The network is trained, by gradient descent as in Chapter 7, to make the reconstruction \(\hat{\mathbf{x}}\) as close as possible to the input \(\mathbf{x}\). No labels are needed: the image is its own target.

Definition 19.3 (Autoencoder) An autoencoder is a neural network made of an encoder, which maps an example \(\mathbf{x}\) to a short code \(\mathbf{z}\) (the bottleneck or latent space), and a decoder, which maps the code back to a reconstruction \(\hat{\mathbf{x}}\). It is trained to minimise a reconstruction error between \(\mathbf{x}\) and \(\hat{\mathbf{x}}\).

Because two numbers cannot store 784, the network is forced to keep only what matters most for rebuilding most images: overall brightness, the size and shape of the lung fields, the contrast between lungs and mediastinum. In this sense an autoencoder is a learned, non-linear version of the dimension reduction of Chapter 10: an autoencoder with no activation functions and a squared reconstruction error finds the same low-dimensional subspace as principal component analysis (Baldi and Hornik 1989).

Is a trained autoencoder a generative model? Half of one. The decoder can turn any pair of numbers into an image, so we could pick a point in the latent space and decode it. But which point? A plain autoencoder places its codes wherever is convenient for reconstruction. In our trained autoencoder the test images have codes centred near (−1.51, −0.77), in an irregular cloud, with empty regions in between. Nothing tells us where the decoder has learned to produce realistic images and where it produces nonsense. To generate, we need a latent space with a known shape from which to draw codes. That is what the variational autoencoder adds.

19.3 Variational autoencoders

A variational autoencoder (VAE) makes two changes to the autoencoder (Kingma and Welling 2014; Rezende, Mohamed, and Wierstra 2014).

  1. The encoder proposes a small cloud, not a point. For each image it outputs a mean \(\mathbf{\mu}\) and a standard deviation \(\mathbf{\sigma}\) for each latent coordinate, and the code is drawn from the normal distribution \(\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{\mu}, \mathbf{\sigma}^2)\). Nearby codes must therefore decode to similar images.
  2. The codes are pulled towards one simple shape. A penalty keeps every image’s cloud close to the standard normal distribution \(\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})\), with mean 0 and standard deviation 1 in each coordinate. After training, the clouds of all the images together fill that shape, so a code drawn from \(\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})\) lands where the decoder knows what to do.

Definition 19.4 (Variational autoencoder) A variational autoencoder (VAE) is an autoencoder whose encoder outputs the parameters \((\mathbf{\mu}, \mathbf{\sigma})\) of a normal distribution over the code, whose code is sampled as \(\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{\mu} + \mathbf{\sigma} \odot \mathbf{\varepsilon}\) with \(\mathbf{\varepsilon} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})\), and whose loss adds to the reconstruction error a Kullback–Leibler penalty that pulls each code distribution towards \(\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})\). New examples are generated by decoding codes drawn from \(\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})\).

19.3.1 The loss: reconstruction plus closeness

For one image the VAE minimises

\[ \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x}) \;=\; \underbrace{\text{reconstruction error}(\mathbf{x}, \hat{\mathbf{x}})}_{\text{look like the input}} \;+\; \underbrace{\mathrm{KL}\big(\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{\mu}, \mathbf{\sigma}^2) \,\|\, \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})\big)}_{\text{stay close to the standard normal}} . \qquad(19.1)\]

The second term is the Kullback–Leibler divergence (KL) (Kullback and Leibler 1951), a measure of how different one probability distribution is from another: zero when they are identical, larger the more they differ. (It is a close relative of the entropy of Chapter 2, and you met it as the quantity that t-SNE minimises in Chapter 10.) For one latent coordinate with mean \(\mu\) and standard deviation \(\sigma\), compared with the standard normal, it has a simple formula:

\[ \mathrm{KL} = \tfrac12\left(\mu^2 + \sigma^2 - \log \sigma^2 - 1\right) . \qquad(19.2)\]

Read it term by term. \(\mu^2\) grows if the code wanders away from 0. \(\sigma^2 - \log\sigma^2 - 1\) is zero at \(\sigma = 1\) and grows if the cloud becomes either a pinpoint (\(\sigma \to 0\), which would turn the VAE back into a plain autoencoder) or too wide. The two terms of Equation 19.1 pull in opposite directions: reconstruction wants every image to have its own precise, well separated code; the KL term wants all codes to overlap in one standard bell. The balance between them is what makes the latent space both informative and fillable. Minimising Equation 19.1 is equivalent to maximising a lower bound on the likelihood of the data, the evidence lower bound (ELBO), which is where the word variational comes from (Kingma and Welling 2019).

19.3.2 The reparameterisation trick

There is a technical obstacle. Training needs gradients (Chapter 7), and you cannot take the gradient of “draw a random number from \(\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)\)” with respect to \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\). The trick of Kingma and Welling (2014) is to move the randomness outside:

\[ z = \mu + \sigma \cdot \varepsilon, \qquad \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1) . \qquad(19.3)\]

The noise \(\varepsilon\) is drawn once and treated as a fixed input; \(z\) is then an ordinary function of \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\), and gradients flow through it. The encoder usually outputs \(\log \sigma^2\) rather than \(\sigma\) (any real number is a valid log-variance, while \(\sigma\) must be positive), so \(\sigma = \exp(\tfrac12 \log\sigma^2)\).1

19.3.3 A VAE that has seen 4 708 chest X-rays

We trained a small VAE on the 4 708 training images of PneumoniaMNIST (script): an encoder with one hidden layer of 400 neurons, a two-dimensional latent space, a mirror-image decoder, 30 passes through the data, well under a minute on one processor core. Two dimensions are far too few for a good generator, but they have one great advantage: you can walk around the whole latent space with two sliders. The same network trained without the KL term (a plain autoencoder) serves as a comparison.

Three things are worth noticing. First, the latent space is continuous: small moves give small changes, and the walk from normal to pneumonia passes through believable intermediate images. Second, the two coordinates have found something real without being told: \(z_1\) mostly changes the clarity of the lung fields, and \(z_2\) the overall brightness and contrast. The diagnoses overlap heavily but are not mixed at random: the normal centre sits at \(z_1 \approx\) 0.90, the pneumonia centre at \(z_1 \approx\) -0.15. Be careful with that observation: a dimension that separates diagnoses might reflect the disease, or how and where the images were taken; the shortcut learning of Chapter 7 applies to generative models too. Third, the images are blurry. A VAE that is unsure which of several sharp images to produce tends to produce their average; with only two latent coordinates it is very unsure. Blur is the classic weakness of VAEs (Kingma and Welling 2019).

What does the KL term buy? Figure 19.2 decodes the same 16 random draws from \(\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})\) with both networks. The VAE was trained so that this is where its codes live, and it produces plausible, if blurry, X-rays. The plain autoencoder reconstructs slightly better, with a mean absolute error of 17.3 grey levels (out of 255) on the test images, against 18.0 for the VAE, but its codes live elsewhere, so the same random draws land in regions it was never trained to fill. With only two latent dimensions the difference is modest, yet more of its images are grainy or over-exposed, and nothing in its training makes such draws safe. Reconstruction is not generation.

Real image generators use many more latent dimensions and convolutional layers (Chapter 7), and produce far sharper pictures. The principle is the same: an encoder that compresses, a decoder that generates, and a penalty that makes the latent space a place you can sample from.

19.4 Generative adversarial networks

In 2014 Goodfellow et al. (2014) proposed a completely different way to train a generator: set it against an opponent. A generative adversarial network (GAN) has two neural networks (Figure 19.3).

  • The generator \(G\) takes random noise \(\mathbf{z}\) and turns it into a fake example \(G(\mathbf{z})\), say an X-ray.
  • The discriminator \(D\) receives an example (sometimes a real X-ray, sometimes a fake one) and outputs the probability \(D(\mathbf{x})\) that it is real. It is simply a classifier, like the networks of Chapters 5 and 7: a discriminative model working for a generative one.

The two are trained in alternation. The discriminator learns to tell real from fake; the generator learns, through the discriminator’s gradients, to produce fakes the discriminator accepts. The original paper compares them to counterfeiters and the police: each improves because the other does. In symbols, they play the game

\[ \min_G \max_D \; \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x} \sim \text{data}}\big[\log D(\mathbf{x})\big] + \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}}\big[\log\big(1 - D(G(\mathbf{z}))\big)\big] . \qquad(19.4)\]

The discriminator wants \(D\) close to 1 on real images and close to 0 on fakes, which makes the expression large; the generator wants its fakes to score high, which makes it small. The first term is exactly the log-likelihood of a logistic classifier (Chapter 5). If both players reach the best they can do, the generator’s distribution equals the data distribution and the discriminator can only guess, \(D = \tfrac12\) everywhere (Goodfellow et al. 2014).

Definition 19.5 (Generative adversarial network) A generative adversarial network (GAN) consists of a generator \(G\), which maps random noise \(\mathbf{z}\) to synthetic examples, and a discriminator \(D\), a classifier that estimates the probability that an example is real. They are trained in alternation, \(D\) to separate real from synthetic examples and \(G\) to make \(D\) classify its examples as real (Equation 19.4).

GANs produce strikingly sharp images, with no averaging blur, because a blurry fake is easy for the discriminator to catch. In medicine they have been used, for example, to synthesise liver-lesion images that improved a CT classifier trained on a small dataset (Frid-Adar et al. 2018). But the game is hard to train. If the discriminator becomes too good, its gradients vanish and the generator stops learning; if the two keep chasing each other, training oscillates instead of settling (Salimans et al. 2016; Arjovsky, Chintala, and Bottou 2017).

The best-known failure is mode collapse. A mode is a peak of the data distribution: a kind of example, such as normal X-rays of toddlers, or pneumonia X-rays with dense consolidation. A generator that has found one kind of fake the discriminator accepts can keep producing variations of it and ignore the others. Every individual image looks perfect; the collection is far too narrow. For a synthetic training set this is dangerous precisely because it is invisible image by image: a collapsed generator of chest X-rays might never produce a pleural effusion, or a child with a central line, and a classifier trained on its output would never learn them. Variants of the GAN loss reduce the problem (Arjovsky, Chintala, and Bottou 2017), and the only reliable defence is to measure diversity across many samples, as we do in Section 19.6.

19.5 Diffusion models

The third family starts from a disarmingly simple observation: it is easy to destroy an image by adding noise, a little at a time, until nothing is left but static. If a network can learn to undo one small step of that destruction, it can be applied over and over, starting from pure static, until an image appears. That is a diffusion model. The idea was proposed by Sohl-Dickstein et al. (2015), inspired by the physics of diffusion, and made practical for images by Ho, Jain, and Abbeel (2020).

19.5.1 The forward process: noise, a little at a time

At each step \(t = 1, \dots, T\) the image is shrunk slightly and a little Gaussian noise is added:

\[ \mathbf{x}_t = \sqrt{1 - \beta_t}\,\mathbf{x}_{t-1} + \sqrt{\beta_t}\,\mathbf{\varepsilon}_t, \qquad \mathbf{\varepsilon}_t \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I}) , \qquad(19.5)\]

where the noise schedule \(\beta_1, \dots, \beta_T\) is a fixed list of small numbers. Ho, Jain, and Abbeel (2020) used \(T = 1\,000\) steps with \(\beta_t\) rising evenly from 0.0001 to 0.02.2 Because a sum of normal variables is normal, the steps can be collapsed into one jump straight from the clean image \(\mathbf{x}_0\) to step \(t\):

2 Elsewhere in this book \(\beta\) denotes regression coefficients (Chapter 4) and \(\alpha\) the elastic-net mixing parameter. In this section only, \(\beta_t\) and \(\bar\alpha_t\) are the noise schedule, as in every paper on diffusion models.

\[ \mathbf{x}_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\mathbf{x}_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\,\mathbf{\varepsilon}, \qquad \bar\alpha_t = \prod_{s=1}^{t} (1 - \beta_s) . \qquad(19.6)\]

\(\bar\alpha_t\) is the share of the original signal’s variance that survives after \(t\) steps; it starts at 1 and falls towards 0. The image is a weighted mix of signal and noise, with weights \(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\) and \(\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\). With the schedule of Ho et al., signal and noise carry equal variance (\(\bar\alpha_t = 0.5\)) at about step 260, and after 1 000 steps \(\bar\alpha_T \approx\) 4e-05: nothing of the X-ray is left. The forward process has no parameters to learn. It is a fixed recipe for destruction.

function gaussNoise(seed, n) {
  let s = seed >>> 0;
  const rnd = () => { s = (s + 0x6D2B79F5) | 0; let t = Math.imul(s ^ s >>> 15, 1 | s); t = t + Math.imul(t ^ t >>> 7, 61 | t) ^ t; return ((t ^ t >>> 14) >>> 0) / 4294967296; };
  const out = new Float64Array(n);

  return out;
}

function pearson(a, b) {

19.5.2 The reverse process: learning to denoise

To generate, we must run the film backwards: from noise \(\mathbf{x}_T\) to an image \(\mathbf{x}_0\). Each backward step, from \(\mathbf{x}_t\) to \(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}\), is learned by a neural network \(\mathbf{\varepsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t)\) (the subscript \(\theta\) stands for its weights) whose only job is to look at a noisy image and estimate the noise in it. Training is remarkably plain (Ho, Jain, and Abbeel 2020):

  1. take a real training image \(\mathbf{x}_0\), a random step \(t\) and fresh noise \(\mathbf{\varepsilon}\);
  2. make the noisy image \(\mathbf{x}_t\) with Equation 19.6;
  3. ask the network for its estimate of the noise and penalise the squared error,

\[ \mathcal{L} = \big\lVert \mathbf{\varepsilon} - \mathbf{\varepsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t) \big\rVert^2 ; \qquad(19.7)\]

  1. repeat, millions of times.

It is ordinary supervised learning (the input is a noisy image, the label is the noise), with labels manufactured for free by the forward process. To generate a new image, start from pure noise and apply the trained network a step at a time: estimate the noise, remove a little of it, add back a smaller amount of fresh noise, and move to the previous step, \(T\) times.

Definition 19.6 (Diffusion model) A diffusion model defines a fixed forward process that gradually adds Gaussian noise to an example over \(T\) steps until only noise remains (Equation 19.6), and learns a reverse process: a neural network trained to predict the noise in a noisy example (Equation 19.7). New examples are generated by starting from pure noise and denoising step by step.

Diffusion models combine much of what the other two families offer: sharp, diverse samples like the best GANs, and stable training with a simple loss like a VAE. Their price is speed: generating one image takes many passes through the network. A common remedy runs the diffusion in the small latent space of an autoencoder instead of on the pixels, latent diffusion, and many of today’s image generators work this way (Rombach et al. 2022). In medical imaging, diffusion models are used to synthesise images, to reconstruct images from fewer measurements, to remove noise and to translate between modalities (Kazerouni et al. 2023).

19.6 Synthetic data in health

Back to HomeBeat. What the statistician proposes has a name.

Definition 19.7 (Synthetic data) Synthetic data are data produced by a generative model (learned from real data, written from expert knowledge, or both) rather than measured on real people or objects. They are meant to reproduce chosen properties of the real data, for a stated purpose, without being a record of any real individual.

Synthetic data are used in health for several purposes (Chen et al. 2021; Jordon et al. 2022): sharing data that could not otherwise leave an institution, as HomeBeat hopes; augmenting a small training set or a rare class, as Emma’s vendor proposes; testing software and pipelines on realistic records before real ones are touched; the Synthea project generates complete, fictional electronic health records from models of disease progression for exactly this purpose (Walonoski et al. 2018); simulating scenarios that have not happened; and teaching: this book’s datasets are synthetic for that reason.

Whatever the purpose, three properties must be checked (Alaa et al. 2022; Jordon et al. 2022):

Definition 19.8 (Fidelity, diversity and privacy) Fidelity: synthetic records look like real ones: individually plausible, with the right distributions and the right relationships between variables. Diversity (or coverage): the synthetic data cover the whole range of the real data, including rare kinds of records, not just the most typical ones. Privacy: the synthetic data do not reveal information about the real individuals used to train the generator; in particular, they do not contain copies or near-copies of real records. A fourth property, utility, asks whether an analysis done on synthetic data gives the same answer as on real data.

The three pull against each other. The highest fidelity is achieved by copying the real data, which destroys privacy; the safest data are random noise, which have no fidelity. Every generator sits somewhere on that trade-off, and only measurement tells you where.

19.6.1 A generator you can write in ten lines: the Gaussian copula

For a table like HomeBeat, a neural network is not the only option. A classic statistical generator, the Gaussian copula, separates a joint distribution into its marginals (the distribution of each variable on its own) and its dependence: how the variables move together (Sklar 1959; Nelsen 2006). It is a standard baseline in synthetic-data software (Patki, Wedge, and Veeramachaneni 2016). The recipe:

  1. For each variable, replace every value by its rank, scaled to lie between 0 and 1, and convert it to a normal score with the normal quantile function (qnorm in R). Each column now looks standard normal, whatever its original shape.
  2. Compute the correlation matrix of the normal scores. This captures the dependence.
  3. Draw as many new rows as you like from a multivariate normal distribution with that correlation.
  4. Convert each new column back through the normal distribution function (pnorm) to a number between 0 and 1, and read off the matching quantile of the real variable. The new values have the real marginal distributions, and the real correlations, approximately.

You will write it yourself in the Lab. We fitted it, and three other generators, to the 400 training participants of the book’s fixed HomeBeat split (data/splits/homebeat_split.csv, the same split as in Chapter 8), keeping its 100 test participants aside, never used by any generator (script). Each generator produced 400 synthetic participants with all nine variables:

  • Independent columns: each variable resampled on its own. Perfect marginals, no relationships: a deliberately bad baseline.
  • Gaussian copula: the recipe above.
  • Tabular VAE: a small VAE (four latent dimensions) on the standardised table, the tabular cousin of the X-ray network (L. Xu et al. 2019).
  • Copy and jitter: real participants copied with 2 % noise added. It looks perfect. It is the memorising generator that every privacy check exists to catch.

19.6.2 How to judge synthetic data

Each column of Table 19.2 answers one question.

  • Marginals. For each variable, the largest gap between the cumulative distributions of real and synthetic values (the Kolmogorov–Smirnov statistic (Massey 1951)), averaged over the nine variables: 0 means identical distributions.
  • Correlation gap. The average absolute difference between the real and synthetic correlation matrices. Fidelity of relationships.
  • Precision and recall. For each real participant, draw a small ball reaching to their fifth nearest real neighbour (on standardised variables). Precision is the share of synthetic records that fall inside some real ball: are the fakes realistic? Recall does the reverse with balls around synthetic records: is every kind of real participant represented? These are fidelity and diversity, measured on whole records (Kynkäänniemi et al. 2019).
  • Utility. Fit a linear regression of the stress score on the other signals using the synthetic data, then measure its error (RMSE) on the 100 real held-out participants: train on synthetic, test on real (Esteban, Hyland, and Rätsch 2017).
  • Privacy: distance to the closest record (DCR). For each synthetic record, the distance to the nearest real training participant. The yardstick is how close a new real person (one of the 100 held out) typically comes to a training participant. A synthetic record much closer than that is suspicious; too close counts records nearer than the closest 5 % of held-out people.

19.6.3 Synthetic is not the same as anonymous

The copy-and-jitter generator is a caricature, but real generators can memorise too. A flexible model trained too long on too few records (exactly the overfitting of Definition 1.8) can reproduce individual training examples, and outliers are the most exposed: the one 98-year-old in a trial, the patient with a rare combination of conditions. Researchers have recovered near-exact copies of individual training images from large diffusion models (Carlini et al. 2023), and attacks on synthetic tables can often infer whether a particular person’s record was in the training data (membership inference) (Stadler, Oprisanu, and Troncoso 2022). A distance check like the one in the widget catches crude copying; it does not prove that nothing can be learned about anyone.

The rigorous answer is differential privacy: a mathematical guarantee that the generator’s output would be almost the same whether or not any one person’s record had been used, achieved by adding carefully calibrated noise during training (Dwork and Roth 2014). It comes at a cost in fidelity, largest for small datasets and rare subgroups: the trade-off again, now with a dial.

The law follows the same logic. Under the GDPR, information that no longer relates to an identifiable person is outside its scope, but data that could be linked back to someone remain personal data (European Parliament and Council of the European Union 2016, Recital 26). Training a generator on patient records is itself processing personal data, and whether its output is anonymous is a question to be assessed and documented, not assumed (Jordon et al. 2022).

19.7 Molecules

Generative models are not limited to images and tables. One of their most consequential uses is in drug discovery, where the “data” are molecules. The number of small molecules that could plausibly be drugs has been estimated at around \(10^{60}\) (Bohacek, McMartin, and Guida 1996), far more than could ever be made or tested. Chemists have always explored this space with intuition and analogy; a generative model can propose candidates at a scale no team can match.

A molecule can be written as a string (in the SMILES notation, aspirin is CC(=O)OC1=CC=CC=C1C(=O)O (Weininger 1988)) or as a graph of atoms joined by bonds. Both can be modelled with the tools of this chapter. A VAE trained on SMILES strings maps molecules into a continuous latent space, where moving smoothly changes the molecule and properties such as solubility can be optimised by searching the space (Gómez-Bombarelli et al. 2018): the latent walk you took with X-rays, with molecules instead. Generative models combined with reinforcement learning (Chapter 14) have designed kinase inhibitors that were synthesised and shown to be active within weeks (Zhavoronkov et al. 2019), and diffusion models now design new protein structures and binders that have been confirmed experimentally (Watson et al. 2023).

What a generative model produces is a hypothesis, not a drug. Most generated molecules cannot be made, would not reach their target, or would be toxic. So generation is always followed by filtering (Figure 19.5): rules of thumb such as Lipinski’s rule of five for oral drugs (molecular weight up to 500, few hydrogen-bond donors and acceptors, moderate fat-solubility (Lipinski et al. 1997)) and predictive models of binding, toxicity and pharmacokinetics. Here the complementarity of Chapter 1 becomes literal: the generative model proposes, discriminative models screen, and experiments decide.

Chapter 18 follows one such molecule all the way. Rentosertib, an inhibitor of the protein kinase TNIK for idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, combined a target found with predictive models and a molecule designed with generative chemistry (Ren et al. 2025). It then had to clear the same hurdles as any other drug: laboratory and animal studies, and a randomised, placebo-controlled phase 2a trial in 71 patients over 12 weeks whose primary endpoint was safety (Z. Xu et al. 2025). The generative model shortened the path to the first patient. It did not shorten the evidence: AI can speed up discovery; it does not lower the bar.

19.8 Limits and governance

Generated data and images are persuasive, which is exactly why they need rules. Five risks recur.

  • Hallucinated features. A generator produces what is plausible, not what is true: the same warning Chapter 1 gave for language models. When image-to-image networks were trained on scans in which tumours were over- or under-represented, they learned to add or remove tumour-like features in the images they produced (Cohen, Luck, and Honari 2018). A synthetic or “enhanced” image must never be read as a measurement of the patient.
  • Bias, reproduced and amplified. A generator learns the population it was trained on. Groups that are rare in the training data are generated rarely and poorly, and mode collapse can make them vanish altogether. Augmenting a training set with synthetic data from a biased generator adds more of the same bias.
  • Weak evaluation. There is no single number for “good synthetic data”. Popular image scores, such as the Fréchet Inception Distance (Heusel et al. 2017), compare features from a network trained on everyday photographs, which may not capture what matters in an X-ray. Clinical review of samples, task-specific utility tests and privacy tests are all needed.
  • Feedback loops. Models trained on data produced by earlier models gradually lose the tails of the real distribution (rare cases first), a degradation called model collapse (Shumailov et al. 2024). As generated content spreads, keeping track of which data are real matters more, not less.
  • Validation on synthetic data. A model can be developed with the help of synthetic data, but its performance must be established on real data from the population where it will be used (Chapters 3 and 7). Testing a model on data from the same generator that helped to train it tells you how well it learned the generator, not the patients.

The regulatory frame is forming around the same points. The EU AI Act requires providers of systems that generate synthetic images, audio, video or text to mark the output in a machine-readable way, so that it can be detected as artificially generated (European Parliament and Council of the European Union 2024, Article 50(2)); data protection law applies to the real records used to train a generator (Section 19.6). In practice, the questions in Table 19.3 belong in the documentation of any synthetic dataset, alongside the code and seed that produced it: the same reproducibility that Chapter 1 asked of every analysis.

19.9 Where this fits in the decision loop

Chapter 1 described models as the source of information for link 3 of the decision-quality chain. Generative models work elsewhere too. When they propose molecules, protocols or scenarios, they strengthen link 2, creative alternatives: a set of options larger than any team would have thought of, to be narrowed by evidence. When they produce synthetic data, they act on the Data box itself: they can make data available that could not otherwise be shared, but they can also quietly replace observation with imitation. Two things therefore stay human. Deciding what generated content is for, and whether its fidelity, diversity and privacy are good enough for that purpose, is a value judgement. And every decision about a patient must ultimately rest on evidence from real patients: rentosertib’s trial, not its design software; the X-ray tool’s external validation, not its synthetic test set.

19.12 Summary

  • Discriminative models learn \(P(y \mid \mathbf{x})\) and predict; generative models learn \(p(\mathbf{x})\) or \(p(\mathbf{x}, y)\) and can produce new examples. Every running example of this book was produced by a hand-written generative model.
  • Latent variables are hidden quantities that generate what we observe. An autoencoder compresses examples into a short code and reconstructs them; a VAE makes the code a distribution, pulls it towards \(\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})\) with a KL penalty, and trains through the reparameterisation trick \(z = \mu + \sigma\varepsilon\), so that random codes decode to plausible examples. Its samples tend to be blurry.
  • A GAN trains a generator against a discriminator; it gives sharp samples but is unstable and prone to mode collapse.
  • A diffusion model destroys examples with a fixed forward noising process, \(\mathbf{x}_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\mathbf{x}_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\mathbf{\varepsilon}\), and learns to reverse it by predicting the noise; it gives sharp, diverse samples at the cost of slow generation.
  • Synthetic data must be judged on fidelity, diversity, privacy and utility, all measured against real data. A simple Gaussian copula can be an excellent generator for small tables; a generator that copies real records looks perfect and protects nobody. Synthetic is not automatically anonymous.
  • In drug discovery, generated molecules are hypotheses, filtered by rules, predictive models and experiments; rentosertib shows that AI can shorten discovery but not the evidence.
  • Generated content can hallucinate, reproduce bias, degrade through feedback loops and mislead if used for validation. It must be labelled, documented and kept apart from real data.
