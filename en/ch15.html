Tonight on Ward 7, the pharmacist’s list

It is still the evening on which Mrs Aoife Byrne, 71, was admitted with sepsis, together with Tomás, 54, and Priya, 38. Each of them already has a short admission note: a few lines of free text written by a busy doctor, listing the antibiotics started tonight and anything that happened after the first doses. Seán, the ward pharmacist, has a job that nobody writes about. He reads notes looking for suspected adverse drug reactions (a rash after an antibiotic, a rise in creatinine blamed on vancomycin, ringing in the ears on gentamicin) so that they can be acted on, recorded and reported; once a month he goes through the archive of discharge summaries for the same purpose. Across the corridor, the safety team of the ONCO-1 trial does the same with the adverse-event notes of patients receiving chemotherapy.

There are 450 discharge summaries in the Ward 7 archive and 400 ONCO-1 notes. Seán reads perhaps twenty an hour, on top of tonight’s new notes. Dr Emma Walsh, the intensivist, asks him: could a computer read them first, and tell you which ones to look at? To answer, we need to turn text, the most human kind of data, into numbers a model can use. That is what this chapter is about.

How to use this chapter

Starting here?

This chapter borrows a few ideas from earlier chapters. In brief:

  • Instances, features and the train/test split (Definition 1.7, Section 1.8). A dataset has one row per instance and one column per feature; a model is built on a training set and judged on a test set it has never seen. Here each document is an instance, and every Ward 7 and ONCO-1 note follows the book-wide split of its patient (Chapter 3).
  • Sensitivity, specificity and predictive values (Chapter 2). Sensitivity is the share of true cases a test catches; specificity the share of non-cases it correctly leaves alone; the positive predictive value (PPV) the share of flagged cases that are real.
  • Logistic regression, ROC/AUC and thresholds (Chapter 5). A logistic model turns features into a probability \(\hat p\); the area under the ROC curve (AUC) measures how well it ranks cases above non-cases; a threshold \(t\) turns \(\hat p\) into an action, and the best threshold follows from the harms of the two errors, \(t^{*} = C_{\text{FP}}/(C_{\text{FP}}+C_{\text{FN}})\). Ridge penalties (Chapter 4) keep a model with many features from overfitting.
  • Cosine similarity (Chapter 10). Two profiles are compared by the angle between them, not by their length: a profile and the same profile doubled have cosine similarity 1.

15.1 Language is symbols; computers need numbers

We use language long before we can describe it. Natural languages such as English, Portuguese or Chinese, the mathematical language of equations and the programming languages of this book, R and Python, look very different, but they share one property: they encode information in symbols governed by rules, and they work in layers: symbols build structures, structures carry meaning, meaning serves an intention, and all of it depends on context (Figure 15.1).

Computers, however, do not handle symbols. They handle numbers, and ultimately binary numbers, sequences of 0s and 1s. Before a computer can do anything with a discharge summary, the text has to become numbers. The first step is to store it as a string.

Definition 15.1 (String and character encoding) A string is an ordered sequence of characters: letters, digits, spaces, punctuation and other symbols. A character encoding maps each character to a number and each number to bytes in memory. Today’s standard is Unicode, which gives every character of every writing system a number called a code point (The Unicode Consortium 2023), stored most often in the UTF-8 encoding, which uses one byte for the basic Latin letters and two to four bytes for everything else (Yergeau 2003).

Take the name of one of tonight’s patients. Table 15.1 shows what the computer actually holds.

Five characters, 6 bytes. The same holds for other scripts: the Chinese sentence 病人报告胸痛 (“the patient reports chest pain”) has 6 characters but takes 18 bytes. Encoding mistakes are mundane but real: a hospital system that assumes one byte per character turns Tomás into TomÃ¡s, and a search for his name then fails.

Meaning does not live inside the machine. It has to be reconstructed, by computation, from a chain of representations:

\[ \text{meaning} \;\rightarrow\; \text{symbols} \;\rightarrow\; \text{code points} \;\rightarrow\; \text{bits}. \]

Once text is stored as an ordered, indexed sequence, a computer can measure its length, search it, cut it and compare it, all without understanding a word. In R, nchar(), grepl(), gsub() and substr() do exactly that; in Python, len(), in, str.replace() and slicing. The rest of the chapter follows the path

\[ \text{language} \;\rightarrow\; \text{numbers} \;\rightarrow\; \text{vectors} \;\rightarrow\; \text{models} \;\rightarrow\; \text{decisions}, \]

and Chapter 16 adds the next link, probability distributions over words.

15.2 What is natural language processing?

Natural language processing (NLP) is the discipline that builds computational systems able to work with human language, written or spoken: to classify it, extract facts from it, search it, translate it and generate it (Jurafsky and Martin 2026). It draws on linguistics, computer science, statistics and machine learning, and its practical promise in health care is to turn unstructured text into structured information (Figure 15.2). That promise matters because so much of what clinicians know about a patient is written, not coded: symptoms, reasons for stopping a drug, suspected reactions and plans are typically found in the free text of the record (Meystre et al. 2008; Wang et al. 2018).

A collection of texts assembled for analysis is a corpus (plural corpora). Language models are trained on corpora of billions of words (encyclopaedias, web pages, books, and in medicine the abstracts and articles of PubMed), and what they learn depends on what those corpora contain. Our corpus is much smaller and entirely synthetic, but it has the shape of the real thing (Table 15.2). Each Ward 7 note is attached to one of the 1 250 simulated Ward 7 patients and each ONCO-1 note to a simulated trial participant, so every note inherits its patient’s train/test assignment. Two pharmacists labelled, in our story, whether each note reports a suspected adverse drug reaction; the leaflet sentences carry no label and are used only for searching.

Aoife’s, Tomás’s and Priya’s admission notes, written tonight, are kept apart: they are the notes the finished system will have to read, and they say nothing yet about how their stay will end. Here is Priya’s:

Ms Priya Nair, 38, admitted to ICU tonight with pneumonia with septic shock. Vancomycin 1500 mg and gentamicin 5 mg/kg given, with a noradrenaline infusion. Flushing and itching of the face within an hour of starting vancomycin, probably related to vancomycin. Infusion slowed and treated with an antihistamine. Reported to the pharmacy team.

A sentence can be represented in more than one way. As a sequence, it is a row of characters or words in order. As a tree, it is nested phrases: [the patient] [reports [chest pain]]. As a graph, it is a set of relations: reports has subject patient and object pain, and pain is modified by chest. The same surface can hide several structures (“the doctor saw the patient with the stethoscope”: who had the stethoscope?), and much of the difficulty of NLP is ambiguity (Jurafsky and Martin 2026). In this chapter we stay with the simplest representation, the sequence, and see how far it takes us.

15.3 Finding patterns: regular expressions

The simplest way to make text useful is to search it. Seán’s first idea is to find every note that mentions a rash.

Definition 15.2 (Regular expression) A regular expression (regex) is a compact language for describing patterns in text, used to find, extract or replace the fragments of a string that match the pattern (Friedl 2006).

A handful of symbols covers most clinical uses (Table 15.3).

One more symbol completes the kit: a vertical bar means or, so (rash|hives|urticaria) matches any of the three words.

In our labelled notes, the pattern \\brash\\b (ignoring capitals) matches 181 notes. Only 70 of them were labelled as reporting a suspected drug reaction. The rest say “no rash”, “skin intact, no rash”, or describe a rash present before any antibiotic. A keyword finds the word, not what is said about it. The v1 edition of this book showed the same with pain: “the patient reports pain”, “the patient denies pain” and “pain was discussed as a possible risk” all match the same pattern.

Regular expressions are also brittle. The same fact can be written in many ways, and each way needs its own pattern. The Ward 7 notes record dates in four styles (12/03/2026, 2026-03-12, 12 March 2026 and 12th of March), and a pattern written for one style finds only those dates:

Together the four patterns find a date in 450 of the 450 notes; any one of them alone misses most. Every new style (“March 12th”, “12.03.26”) needs another rule, and rules start to overlap and conflict. This is the central weakness of hand-written rules, and the reason the rest of the chapter moves towards representations from which a model can learn patterns instead. Regular expressions remain indispensable for well-defined formats (doses, dates, laboratory values, identifiers) and for cleaning text before anything else is done with it.

15.4 “No rash” is not a rash: negation

Clinical text is full of findings that are not there. Doctors write “no rash”, “denies chest pain”, “negative for fever” because the absence of a finding is information: it rules things out. A keyword search counts them all as positives. Detecting them is called negation detection.

Definition 15.3 (Negation detection) Negation detection decides whether a finding mentioned in a text is affirmed (present) or negated (denied, absent, ruled out). Rule-based methods look for trigger phrases (“no”, “denies”, “without”, “negative for”, “ruled out”) and decide whether the finding falls within the trigger’s scope: the stretch of words the trigger applies to.

The classic algorithm is NegEx (Chapman et al. 2001), built for discharge summaries. It uses a list of triggers that come before a finding (“no”, “denies”, “no evidence of”) and a shorter list that come after it (“was ruled out”); a list of pseudo-negations, phrases that contain a trigger but do not negate (“no increase in”, “not only”); and a scope limited to a few words after the trigger (five in the original algorithm) and cut short by termination words such as “but” and “however”. Simple as it is, it was accurate enough to become a standard component of clinical NLP pipelines, and its successor ConText also flags findings that are hypothetical, historical, or experienced by someone other than the patient, as in “family history of penicillin allergy” (Harkema et al. 2009).

negTok = s => (String(s).toLowerCase().match(/[a-z0-9]+|;/g) || [])
negFind = (tok, phrase, start = 0) => {
  const p = String(phrase).toLowerCase().split(/\s+/).filter(x => x);
  if (!p.length) return -1;
  for (let i = start; i <= tok.length - p.length; i++) {
    let ok = true;
    for (let j = 0; j < p.length; j++) if (tok[i + j] !== p[j]) { ok = false; break; }
    if (ok) return i;
  }
  return -1;
}
negex = (sentence, finding, win, usePseudo, useTerm, usePost) => {
  const tok = negTok(sentence), ts = negFind(tok, finding);
  if (ts < 0) return {status: "not found", tok, ts, te: -1, trig: null};
  const te = ts + String(finding).trim().split(/\s+/).length - 1;
  const blocked = (a, b) => useTerm && tok.slice(a, b).some(x => negRules.term.includes(x));
  let i = 0;
  while (i < ts) {
    if (usePseudo && negRules.pseudo.some(q => negFind(tok.slice(i), q) === 0)) { i += 2; continue; }
    const hit = negRules.pre.find(t => negFind(tok.slice(i), t) === 0);
    if (hit) {
      const e = i + hit.split(" ").length - 1;
      if (e < ts && ts - e <= win && !blocked(e + 1, ts)) return {status: "negated", tok, ts, te, trig: [i, e]};
      i = e + 1; continue;
    }
    i++;
  }
  if (usePost) for (const t of negRules.post) {
    const s = negFind(tok, t, te + 1);
    if (s > te && s - te <= win && !blocked(te + 1, s)) return {status: "negated", tok, ts, te, trig: [s, s + t.split(" ").length - 1]};
  }
  return {status: "affirmed", tok, ts, te, trig: null};
}

15.5 Tokens and vocabularies

Regular expressions and negation rules work on the raw string. To learn from text, a model needs discrete units it can count and index. These units are called tokens.

Definition 15.4 (Token, tokenisation and vocabulary) A token is the basic unit into which a text is cut before it is processed: a word, a piece of a word, a character or a byte. Tokenisation is the cutting. The set of distinct tokens a system knows is its vocabulary \(\mathcal{V}\), of size \(\lvert\mathcal{V}\rvert\); each token in it is given an integer token ID, and a text becomes a sequence of tokens \(w_1, w_2, \dots, w_T\), that is, a sequence of IDs.

Figure 15.4 follows one sentence through the steps. The token IDs are only labels (token 4 is not “twice” token 2), so a model cannot use them as numbers directly. The classical solution is the one-hot vector: a vector as long as the vocabulary, with a 1 in the token’s own position and 0 everywhere else. Modern models instead learn a short dense vector for each token, an embedding; how such vectors are learned, and why similar words end up close together, is the subject of Chapter 16.

15.5.1 Words, characters and bytes

The most intuitive choice is word-level tokenisation: split at spaces and punctuation. It is transparent and works well when the vocabulary is stable, but it has two weaknesses. First, the vocabulary keeps growing: our 687 training notes contain 36 930 word tokens but already 376 distinct words (counting numbers such as 1000 as words), and every new drug, misspelling or abbreviation adds more. Second, a word that was never seen in training is out of vocabulary (OOV): a word-level model has no representation for it and usually replaces it with a generic <UNK> token. If we take the vocabulary of the training notes and read the leaflet sentences with it, 130 distinct leaflet words are unknown, dizziness, kidneys, bleeding, drowsy, toothbrush, fits among them, and 52 % of the leaflet’s word tokens become <UNK>. Word tokens also treat treat, treats, treated and treatment as unrelated, so nothing learned about one helps with the others.

Character-level tokenisation goes to the other extreme. The vocabulary is tiny (the training notes use only 44 distinct characters, counting letters, digits, spaces and punctuation), and there is no OOV problem: any new word, misspelling (“paim” for “pain”) or mixed-language note can be spelled out. The price is length. A typical training note has about 54 words but 299 characters, so a model must read many more steps and learn for itself that r-a-s-h is a unit. Byte-level tokenisation takes the idea one step further: 256 possible bytes cover every UTF-8 text in every script, with a fixed vocabulary (Radford et al. 2019). Chinese and accented characters then cost two to four tokens each, so the same message is longer, and more expensive to process, in some languages than in others (Petrov et al. 2023).

15.6 Subwords: byte-pair encoding and its relatives

Between words and characters lies the compromise used by almost every modern language model: subword tokens. Frequent words stay whole; rare words are built from frequent pieces. A model that has never seen immunotherapies but knows immun, o and therapies can still represent it, and what it learns about therapy is shared by chemotherapy and radiotherapy.

Definition 15.5 (Byte-pair encoding (BPE)) Byte-pair encoding builds a subword vocabulary from a corpus. Start with every word split into characters, plus an end-of-word marker. Count every pair of adjacent symbols, weighting each word by its frequency; merge the most frequent pair into a new symbol; repeat for a chosen number of merges. The list of merges, applied in order, then tokenises any new word. BPE began as a data-compression method (Gage 1994) and was adapted to split rare words in neural machine translation (Sennrich, Haddow, and Birch 2016).

The explorer below runs BPE on a tiny corpus of twenty clinical words, each with a frequency. Before any merge, every word is a row of single letters followed by the end-of-word marker _. The first merges join the most common pairs: y + _, then h + e, then r + a. Then, step by step, the pieces of therapy assemble, because therapy, chemotherapy, immunotherapy, radiotherapy and therapies together make it the most frequent string in the corpus.

bpeApply = (sy, a, b) => { const out = []; let i = 0;
  while (i < sy.length) { if (i < sy.length - 1 && sy[i] === a && sy[i + 1] === b) { out.push(a + b); i += 2; } else { out.push(sy[i]); i++; } }
  return out; }
bpeMerges = {
  let words = bpeCorpus.map(([w, c]) => ({w, c, sy: [...w, "_"]}));
  const merges = [];
  for (let step = 1; step <= 100; step++) {
    const counts = new Map();
    for (const x of words) for (let i = 0; i < x.sy.length - 1; i++) {
      const k = x.sy[i] + "\u0001" + x.sy[i + 1]; counts.set(k, (counts.get(k) || 0) + x.c); }
    if (counts.size === 0) break;
    let best = null, bc = -1, ba = "", bb = "";
    for (const [k, c] of counts) { const [a, b] = k.split("\u0001");
      if (c > bc || (c === bc && (a < ba || (a === ba && b < bb)))) { best = k; bc = c; ba = a; bb = b; } }
    merges.push({step, a: ba, b: bb, count: bc});
    words = words.map(x => ({...x, sy: bpeApply(x.sy, ba, bb)}));
  }
  return merges;
}

With all the merges this corpus allows, chemotherapies becomes chem · o · therapies_ (three tokens are reached after 77 merges), whereas cardiotoxic, built from a rare prefix, stays in many pieces: c · ardio · toxic_. Subwords follow frequency, not meaning. They often coincide with real morphemes (therap, toxic), but only when those morphemes are frequent in the training corpus; a tokeniser trained on general English will cut a drug name into fragments no pharmacologist would recognise.

Several algorithms share this idea. WordPiece, introduced for Japanese and Korean voice search (Schuster and Nakajima 2012) and used in Google’s translation system (Wu et al. 2016) and later in BERT (Devlin et al. 2019), chooses each merge by how much it increases the likelihood of the corpus rather than by raw frequency, and marks pieces that continue a word with ##. To tokenise, it takes the longest piece of the vocabulary that matches the start of the word, then the longest matching continuation, and so on. With a vocabulary containing immun, ##o, ##therap and ##ies, the word immunotherapies becomes immun ##o ##therap ##ies; only if no piece matches at some point does the word fall back to [UNK]. The unigram language model tokeniser works in the opposite direction: it starts from a large set of candidate pieces, gives each a probability, and repeatedly prunes the pieces whose removal least reduces the likelihood of the corpus; a word may then be segmented in several ways, and the most probable segmentation is chosen (Kudo 2018). It is available, with BPE, in the SentencePiece library (Kudo and Richardson 2018). GPT-2 applied BPE to bytes instead of characters, so that no input can ever be out of vocabulary (Radford et al. 2019).

The size of the vocabulary is a design choice with consequences. BERT uses about 30 000 WordPiece tokens (Devlin et al. 2019) and GPT-2 about 50 000 byte-level BPE tokens (Radford et al. 2019). A larger vocabulary means shorter sequences but more parameters, because each token needs its own row in the model’s embedding table and its own output probability; a smaller one means longer sequences.

15.7 Bag of words

We can now turn a whole document into a vector. The oldest and still useful way is to count.

Definition 15.6 (Bag of words and document-term matrix) A bag-of-words representation describes a document by how many times each vocabulary word occurs in it, ignoring the order of the words. Stacking the vectors of \(n\) documents gives the document-term matrix: \(n\) rows (documents, our instances), \(\lvert\mathcal{V}\rvert\) columns (words, our features), and in each cell the count of word \(w\) in document \(i\). Most cells are zero: the matrix is sparse.

In the language of Definition 1.7, each note becomes a row \(\mathbf{x}_i\) and each vocabulary word a feature. The training notes give a matrix with 687 rows and 376 columns, almost all of them zeros in any given row. Bag-of-words vectors made the first scalable text classifiers possible (spam filters, document routing, search engines (Manning, Raghavan, and Schütze 2008)), but the price of ignoring order is easy to see. Here are two sentences with opposite clinical meanings:

Their vectors are identical, so their cosine similarity is 1.00: to a bag of words they are the same document. One remedy is to count pairs of adjacent words (bigrams) as extra features: “no fever” and “no rash” are different pairs. Another is to mark negated words before counting, turning “no rash” into a token NEG_rash (Pang, Lee, and Vaithyanathan 2002). Both appear in our classifier below. The deeper remedy, representations that read words in context, is the story of Chapters 16 and 17.

15.8 TF-IDF: weighting words by what they tell you

Raw counts treat every word as equally informative. But a word that appears in almost every note (the, with, creatinine in an ICU archive) says little about any particular note, whereas a word that appears in a few (hives, tinnitus) says a lot. TF-IDF formalises this intuition (Spärck Jones 1972; Salton and Buckley 1988).

Definition 15.7 (TF-IDF) For a collection of \(n\) documents, the term frequency \(\text{tf}(w, i)\) is the number of times word \(w\) occurs in document \(i\); the document frequency \(\text{df}(w)\) is the number of documents that contain \(w\) at least once; the inverse document frequency is \[ \text{idf}(w) = \log \frac{n}{\text{df}(w)}, \] and the TF-IDF weight of \(w\) in document \(i\) is \[ \text{tfidf}(w, i) = \text{tf}(w, i) \times \text{idf}(w). \qquad(15.1)\] A word that occurs in every document has \(\text{idf} = \log 1 = 0\) and weight 0; a word frequent in one document and rare elsewhere gets a large weight.

The idea of weighting a term by how specific it is, rather than how frequent, is due to Karen Spärck Jones (Spärck Jones 1972); it became, with term frequency, the standard weighting of search engines for decades (Salton and Buckley 1988; Manning, Raghavan, and Schütze 2008). Table 15.6 works it out on four miniature notes.

Vancomycin occurs twice in note B and in only two of the four notes, so it gets the largest weight in B. No occurs in three notes and carries little weight anywhere; nausea and piperacillin occur in only one note each and are the most specific words of the collection. Software packages use small variants of Equation 15.1. scikit-learn, for example, adds one to the numerator and the denominator of the idf fraction and one to the logarithm (“smoothing”, so that no weight is exactly zero), and by default rescales every document vector to length 1 (Pedregosa et al. 2011). The ranking of words changes little; the exact numbers do, so always say which variant you used.

15.9 Searching notes: cosine similarity

With every document a vector, finding documents becomes geometry. A query, a few words typed by Seán, is turned into a vector in the same way, and the documents whose vectors point in the most similar direction are returned first.

Definition 15.8 (Cosine similarity of documents) The cosine similarity of two document vectors is the cosine of the angle between them, \[ \cos(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{x}_{i'}) = \frac{\sum_{w} x_{iw}\, x_{i'w}}{\sqrt{\sum_w x_{iw}^2}\,\sqrt{\sum_w x_{i'w}^2}} , \qquad(15.2)\] where the sums run over the vocabulary. For count or TF-IDF vectors, which have no negative entries, it ranges from 0 (no word in common) to 1 (the same proportions of the same words). Because it ignores the length of the vectors, a long note and a short note on the same topic can still be very similar (Manning, Raghavan, and Schütze 2008, ch. 6).

This is the cosine distance of Chapter 10 turned into a similarity, \(1 - d_{\cos}\), and the reason for choosing it is the same: there, two skin swabs sequenced at different depths had the same profile; here, a two-line note and a twenty-line note about the same reaction should be neighbours.

15.10 A first text classifier: flagging adverse drug reactions

Search answers Seán’s questions one at a time. What he really wants is a list, every morning, of the notes most likely to report a suspected adverse drug reaction (ADR): a harmful, unintended response to a medicine that warrants changing treatment, monitoring or future prescribing (Edwards and Aronson 2000). Reactions found in clinical notes are a recognised and under-used source of safety signals, and finding them automatically is a long-standing goal of clinical NLP (Harpaz et al. 2014; Wang et al. 2018).

Definition 15.9 (Text classifier) A text classifier is a supervised model whose input is a document, represented as a vector (for example TF-IDF weights), and whose output is a class or, better, a probability for each class. It is trained on documents whose class was assigned by people (the labels) and evaluated on documents it has not seen.

Nothing here is new except the input. The 687 training notes become a TF-IDF matrix; the label is 1 if the pharmacists judged that the note reports a suspected ADR (34 % of labelled notes) and 0 otherwise; and a logistic regression with a ridge penalty (Cessie and Houwelingen 1992) (Chapter 5, Chapter 4) estimates the probability \(\hat p\) that a note reports one. With hundreds of word features and a few hundred notes, the penalty is not optional: without it, the model would memorise the training notes. The fitted model is evaluated on the 163 test notes. Table 15.7 compares it with two rule-based screens, a keyword list of reaction words (rash, hives, nausea, neutropenia, …) and the same list with NegEx-lite ignoring negated mentions.

The keyword list catches 61 % of the reactions but flags so many notes without one that its PPV is only 0.41: reaction words appear just as often in notes saying “rash after gardening, unrelated to the study drug”. NegEx-lite removes the “no rash” mentions and raises the PPV to 0.57, at the cost of some sensitivity. The learned models do far better, with an AUC of 0.974 for single words and 0.976 when word pairs are added. Marking negations changes nothing here: in these notes, what decides the label is not whether a symptom is present but what it is attributed to, and that is carried by words such as related, attributed and unrelated. A different task, “does the patient have a rash?”, would reward negation handling much more.

What has the model learned? Table 15.8 lists the features with the largest coefficients of the model with word pairs (each coefficient is the change in log-odds for one unit of TF-IDF weight).

Most of these make sense: related to, probably related, effect of up; not, unrelated to, the infection down. Others are artefacts of how notes are written: on day rises because the reaction sentences usually say when the reaction started. A model that leans on such phrasing will stumble when a new hospital writes differently. Reading the largest weights is the simplest way to look inside a text classifier, with all the caveats of Chapter 12: a weight is an association with the label in these notes, not a reason.

Finally, the model reads tonight’s notes. Tomás’s (“No adverse drug reactions so far”) gets \(\hat p\) = 0.06; Priya’s, with flushing and itching “probably related to vancomycin”, 0.99; Aoife’s, with a mild itch since the first antibiotic dose, “cause unclear”, 0.49. Aoife’s note is precisely the kind on which two pharmacists might disagree. These are probabilities that each note reports a suspected adverse drug reaction, not the risks of acute kidney injury that the logistic model of Chapter 5 gives the same three patients (0.49 / 0.07 / 0.99): the numbers happen to be close, but they answer a different question, and whether the kidneys will be injured belongs to the models of Part II.

15.11 Who reads what? Thresholds and a review queue

A probability is not yet a decision. In Chapter 5 one threshold split patients into “give the bundle” and “do not”. Here a single threshold would split notes into “report” and “ignore”, and either error is costly: a missed reaction is a safety signal lost; a false report wastes a pharmacovigilance officer’s time and pollutes the database. But Seán has a third option, which a bundle decision on Ward 7 did not: read the note himself.

Definition 15.10 (Review queue) A review queue uses two thresholds on a classifier’s probability. Below \(t_{\text{low}}\) a document is cleared automatically; at or above \(t_{\text{high}}\) it is acted on automatically; in between it is sent to a person. Widening the band between the thresholds trades human workload for fewer automatic errors. Letting a classifier abstain when it is unsure is known as classification with a reject option (Chow 1970).

The two thresholds come from the same reasoning as \(t^{*}\) in Chapter 5, with a third action added. Suppose a missed reaction costs \(C_{\text{FN}}\), a false automatic report \(C_{\text{FP}}\), and a human review \(C_{\text{R}}\), smaller than both (Seán’s few minutes, assuming he then gets the note right). For a note with probability \(\hat p\), clearing it has expected cost \(\hat p\,C_{\text{FN}}\), flagging it \((1-\hat p)\,C_{\text{FP}}\), and reviewing it \(C_{\text{R}}\). Review is the cheapest option when \(\hat p\,C_{\text{FN}} > C_{\text{R}}\) and \((1-\hat p)\,C_{\text{FP}} > C_{\text{R}}\), that is, when \[ t_{\text{low}} = \frac{C_{\text{R}}}{C_{\text{FN}}} \;<\; \hat p \;<\; 1 - \frac{C_{\text{R}}}{C_{\text{FP}}} = t_{\text{high}} . \qquad(15.3)\] The cheaper a review compared with an error, the wider the band. If reading a note costs one twentieth of a missed reaction and one third of a false report, review every note with \(\hat p\) between 0.05 and 0.67. As in Chapter 5, these numbers assume calibrated probabilities, and the costs are value judgements that the pharmacy team, not the software vendor, should make.

A review queue is also how a classifier keeps learning. The notes Seán reads, with his decisions, are new labelled examples from the place the model is used, and the ones in the band are the most informative of all, because the model was unsure about them. Feeding them back is a simple form of active learning (Settles 2009). It has a catch: if only the uncertain notes are ever checked, errors in the automatic streams go unseen. A sensible queue also sends a small random sample of cleared and flagged notes for review, to keep measuring the error rates the thresholds were chosen for.

15.12 Where this fits in the decision loop

This chapter turned the least structured data in the hospital into information for a decision. Text is stored as strings of encoded characters (Section 15.1); regular expressions find well-defined patterns but not meaning (Section 15.3); negation rules catch the commonest way in which a mention means its opposite (Section 15.4); tokens and subwords cut text into countable units (Section 15.5, Section 15.6); bags of words and TF-IDF turn documents into vectors (Section 15.7, Section 15.8), which can be searched by cosine similarity (Section 15.9) and fed to a classifier (Section 15.10).

What can go wrong? Keywords find words, not facts; a bag of words cannot tell “rash, no fever” from “fever, no rash”; a classifier can learn the writing habits of one hospital; search can retrieve the exact opposite of what was asked. Who stays in control? The thresholds of the review queue (Section 15.11) are a value judgement about the cost of a missed reaction, a false report and a pharmacist’s time, and the person reading the uncertain notes is part of the system, not a fallback for it.

4. Cardio occurs only in cardiotoxicity, which has frequency 2, so pairs inside cardio are never among the most frequent. With cardiology at frequency 10, pairs such as c+a, ar and rd would become frequent, and cardio would probably be merged into one token early on.

5. Kidney appears mainly in the leaflet sentences (“can affect your kidneys”) and creatinine in the clinical notes. TF-IDF matches strings, not concepts: it cannot know that kidney and creatinine belong together. Learned embeddings (Chapter 16) and medical vocabularies are two remedies.

7. \(t_{\text{low}} = 1/30 \approx 0.03\) and \(t_{\text{high}} = 1 - 1/4 = 0.75\): the band is wide, because a review is cheap compared with a missed reaction; Seán reads many more notes than with the thresholds 0.10 and 0.65.

15.15 Summary

  • Computers store text as strings of characters, each character a Unicode code point stored as one to four UTF-8 bytes. Meaning is not in the machine; it is reconstructed by computation.
  • Regular expressions find and extract well-defined patterns (doses, dates, identifiers) but are brittle: every new way of writing the same fact needs another rule. A keyword finds a word, not what is said about it.
  • Negation detection (NegEx) uses trigger phrases, a limited scope, pseudo-negations and termination words to decide whether a finding is affirmed or denied. It handles the common patterns and misses the long tail.
  • Tokenisation cuts text into tokens with integer IDs from a vocabulary. Word tokens suffer from out-of-vocabulary words; characters and bytes have none but make sequences long; subwords (BPE, WordPiece, unigram LM) are the modern compromise and follow frequency, not meaning.
  • A bag of words turns a document into a vector of counts, a row of the document-term matrix, and loses word order: “rash, no fever” equals “fever, no rash”. TF-IDF weights words by how specific they are, \(\text{tf} \times \log(n/\text{df})\).
  • Cosine similarity compares documents by direction, not length, and turns search into geometry. Retrieval inherits the blindness of the bag of words.
  • A text classifier is ordinary supervised learning on document vectors: a penalised logistic regression on TF-IDF features clearly beats keyword rules for flagging suspected adverse drug reactions, and its largest weights show what it relies on.
  • A review queue uses two thresholds, \(t_{\text{low}} = C_{\text{R}}/C_{\text{FN}}\) and \(t_{\text{high}} = 1 - C_{\text{R}}/C_{\text{FP}}\), to send only the uncertain notes to a person.
