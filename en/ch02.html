Tonight on Ward 7

It is 10 pm. Dr Emma Walsh, the intensivist on call, and Seán, the ward pharmacist, are still deciding whether to start the kidney-protective bundle for Mrs Aoife Byrne, 71, admitted with sepsis. The model we will build in Chapter 5 puts Aoife’s chance of acute kidney injury (AKI) in the next 48 hours at 49 %. The laboratory offers a rapid urine test for kidney stress. To answer either question you need the language of probability: how to describe what we do not know, how to update it when evidence arrives, and how to turn it into a decision.

2.1 Why decision-makers need probability

At the end of Chapter 1 a straight line predicted that Aoife’s creatinine would rise by about 0.3 mg/dL: right on the AKI threshold. A single number like that hides the question Emma actually needs answered: how likely is AKI? Two patients with the same predicted rise can carry very different risks, depending on how much the outcome varies around the prediction.

Uncertainty in health care has two sources (Spiegelhalter 2019). Some of it is variability: even patients with identical measurements respond differently, as the scatter around your line in Section 1.4 showed. Some of it is lack of knowledge: we have seen only 1 250 past patients, so even the average risk is known only approximately. Probability describes both. It can be read as a long-run frequency (“of 100 patients like Aoife, about 49 develop AKI”) or as a degree of belief (“I am 49 % sure Aoife will develop AKI”); the rules of calculation are the same either way (Blitzstein and Hwang 2019). In this chapter we lean on the frequency reading, because counting people is the most reliable way to reason about chance, and we return to beliefs when we meet Bayes’ theorem.

Probability is the currency of the decision basis of Chapter 1: the model supplies probabilities, people supply values, and the decision combines the two (Section 1.5). Before we can spend that currency, we need to know what it buys.

2.2 Random variables and their distributions

Definition 2.1 (Random variable) A random variable is a quantity whose value is not known in advance and is described by probabilities: whether a patient develops AKI, how many patients on a ward do, a participant’s resting heart rate tomorrow. It is written with a capital letter, \(X\); a value it can take is written in lower case, \(x\). \(P(X = x)\) reads “the probability that \(X\) takes the value \(x\)”.

Definition 2.2 (Probability distribution) The probability distribution of a random variable lists the values it can take and how probable each one is. A discrete variable (a count, a yes/no) has a probability for each value, and these add up to 1. A continuous variable (a heart rate, a concentration) has a density: a curve whose area over an interval is the probability of falling in that interval, and whose total area is 1 (Blitzstein and Hwang 2019).

2.2.1 Yes or no: the Bernoulli distribution

On Ward 7 the simplest random variable is whether a patient develops AKI. Write \(X = 1\) if they do and \(X = 0\) if they do not. If the probability of AKI is \(\theta\), then

\[ P(X = 1) = \theta, \qquad P(X = 0) = 1 - \theta . \qquad(2.1)\]

This is the Bernoulli distribution, the distribution of every yes/no outcome in medicine: AKI or not, response or not, a batch that passes or fails its release test. Of the 1 250 past Ward 7 patients, 725 met the AKI criterion, a proportion of 0.58. Treating every patient as if they had the same \(\theta\) is crude (the point of building models is to give each patient their own \(\theta_i\)), but it is the natural starting point.

2.2.2 Counting yes’s: the binomial distribution

Suppose the ward has 20 patients and each, independently, has the same probability \(\theta =\) 0.58 of AKI. The number \(Y\) who develop AKI is a count between 0 and 20, with a binomial distribution:

\[ P(Y = k) = \binom{n}{k}\, \theta^{k} (1 - \theta)^{n - k}, \qquad k = 0, 1, \dots, n , \qquad(2.2)\]

where \(\theta^{k}(1-\theta)^{n-k}\) is the probability of one particular sequence with \(k\) cases and \(n-k\) non-cases, and \(\binom{n}{k}\) (“\(n\) choose \(k\)”) counts how many such sequences there are. Seán plans renal dose reviews for the next two days: if he can handle 14 patients with AKI, the probability that 15 or more of the 20 patients develop it is \(P(Y \geq 15) =\) 0.092. Not negligible: roughly one time in 11.

Definition 2.3 (Expectation and variance) The expectation (or expected value, mean) of a random variable, \(\mathbb{E}[X]\), is its long-run average: the sum of each value times its probability (for a continuous variable, an integral). The variance, \(\operatorname{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}\big[(X - \mathbb{E}[X])^2\big]\), measures how far values typically fall from that average; its square root is the standard deviation. For a Bernoulli variable \(\mathbb{E}[X] = \theta\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(X) = \theta(1-\theta)\); for a binomial count \(\mathbb{E}[Y] = n\theta\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(Y) = n\theta(1-\theta)\) (Blitzstein and Hwang 2019, ch. 4).

On a ward of 20, Seán should therefore expect about 11.6 cases of AKI, with a standard deviation of about 2.2. Notice that \(\theta(1-\theta)\) is largest at \(\theta = 0.5\): a yes/no outcome is most unpredictable when both answers are equally likely, an idea we turn into a measure of uncertainty in Section 2.6.

2.2.3 Measurements on a scale: the normal distribution

Many measurements are continuous. In the HomeBeat digital trial (500 people wearing a smartwatch at home, Table 1.1), the resting heart rate has mean 69.9 bpm and standard deviation 12.9 bpm. A common model for such a variable is the normal (Gaussian) distribution, with density

\[ f(x \mid \mu, \sigma) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}} \exp\!\left( -\frac{(x - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2} \right), \qquad(2.3)\]

the familiar symmetric bell, centred at the mean \(\mu\) with spread given by the standard deviation \(\sigma\) (Figure 2.1). About 95 % of the area lies within two standard deviations of the mean: for HomeBeat the normal model puts 95 % of participants in that band, and the data actually contain 97 %.

A density is not a probability. The probability of a resting heart rate of exactly 75.000… bpm is zero; what has a probability is an interval, such as “above 80 bpm”, and it equals the area under the curve. Under the normal model, \(P(\text{HR} > 80) =\) 0.218; in the data, 24.2 % of participants are above 80 bpm. The model is good, not perfect: like every model in Chapter 1’s sense, it keeps some features of reality and loses others.

Lena, a HomeBeat participant, records a resting heart rate of 88 bpm. Is that unusual? It lies \(z = (88 - 69.9)/12.9 =\) 1.40 standard deviations above the mean, and the normal model says that about 8 % of participants are at least that high. Unusual, but hardly alarming on its own: whether it should trigger a call depends on what else we know about Lena, which is exactly what Bayes’ theorem will handle.

2.3 Likelihood: which value best explains the data?

So far we have assumed that we know \(\theta\), \(\mu\) or \(\sigma\) and asked what data to expect. Real problems run the other way: we have data and want to learn about the unknown values that produced them. This reverse direction is statistical inference. For Ward 7 the questions are: what is the probability of AKI in this population? How sure can we be, given 1 250 patients? Would we be just as sure with 20?

There are two broad schools of inference (Gelman et al. 2013; Spiegelhalter 2019). In frequentist inference the parameter is a fixed but unknown constant, and randomness comes only from which data happened to be collected; the aim is a good single estimate and a statement of how much it would vary from sample to sample. In Bayesian inference the parameter itself is uncertain and is described by a probability distribution, which is updated as data arrive. The two schools disagree about interpretation, but they share one building block: the likelihood.

Definition 2.4 (Likelihood) Given a probability model and observed data, the likelihood \(L(\theta)\) is the probability (or density) of the observed data, viewed as a function of the parameter \(\theta\) with the data held fixed. It measures how well each candidate value of \(\theta\) explains the data; it is not the probability that \(\theta\) is true. Its logarithm, \(\ell(\theta) = \log L(\theta)\), is the log-likelihood (Fisher 1922).

Take the first 20 Ward 7 patients in a random order, of whom 12 developed AKI. If each patient has probability \(\theta\) of AKI, independently, the probability of exactly this sequence of outcomes is

\[ L(\theta) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} \theta^{x_i} (1-\theta)^{1 - x_i} = \theta^{k} (1-\theta)^{n-k} = \theta^{12} (1-\theta)^{8}, \qquad(2.4)\]

where \(x_i\) is 1 or 0 for patient \(i\) and \(k = \sum x_i\) is the number of cases. At \(\theta = 0.3\) this is about \(3.1 \times 10^{-8}\); at \(\theta = 0.6\) it is about \(1.4 \times 10^{-6}\), some 47 times larger. Both numbers are tiny (any particular sequence of 20 outcomes is improbable), but their ratio says that \(\theta = 0.6\) explains these patients far better than \(\theta = 0.3\). Because products of many small numbers become unmanageably small, we usually work with the log-likelihood, which turns the product into a sum:

\[ \ell(\theta) = k \log\theta + (n-k)\log(1-\theta) . \qquad(2.5)\]

Definition 2.5 (Maximum likelihood estimate) The maximum likelihood estimate (MLE) \(\hat\theta\) is the parameter value with the highest likelihood (equivalently, the highest log-likelihood): \(\hat\theta = \arg\max_\theta L(\theta)\) (Fisher 1922). For a yes/no outcome it is the observed proportion, \(\hat\theta = k/n\); for the mean of a normal distribution with known \(\sigma\) it is the sample mean, \(\hat\mu = \bar y\).

For the curious: why the MLE of a proportion is \(k/n\)

At the top of the curve the slope of \(\ell(\theta)\) is zero. Differentiating Equation 2.5,

\[ \frac{d\ell}{d\theta} = \frac{k}{\theta} - \frac{n-k}{1-\theta} = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad k(1-\theta) = (n-k)\theta \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \hat\theta = \frac{k}{n}. \]

For a normal model with known \(\sigma\), the log-likelihood of the mean is \(\ell(\mu) = -\tfrac{n}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^2) - \tfrac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_i (y_i - \mu)^2\), which is largest when the sum of squared distances \(\sum_i (y_i-\mu)^2\) is smallest, that is, at \(\hat\mu = \bar y\). Minimising squared distances is the least squares idea of Chapter 4: least squares is maximum likelihood under a normal model (Hastie, Tibshirani, and Friedman 2009, ch. 2).

2.4 Bayes’ theorem and the diagnostic test

2.4.1 Updating by counting

We start with a question from the HomeBeat trial. Call a participant highly frail if their frailty index is at least 0.5, and say their watch shows a fast resting heart rate if it is above 85 bpm. A participant’s watch reports a fast heart rate. How likely is it that they are highly frail? Table 2.1 counts the 500 participants.

Among the participants with a fast heart rate, the proportion who are highly frail is read straight from the first column: 34 out of 63, or 0.54. Before seeing the watch, the proportion was 0.23 (the prior); after seeing a fast heart rate it is 0.54 (the posterior). The evidence more than doubled our belief.

We can obtain the same number without the full table, from three quantities that are often all we are given: the prior \(P(F) =\) 0.234, the probability of a fast heart rate among the highly frail, \(P(H \mid F) =\) 0.291, and among the others, \(P(H \mid \text{not } F) =\) 0.076. The vertical bar reads “given”: \(P(H \mid F)\) is the probability of \(H\) among those for whom \(F\) is true. Then

\[ P(F \mid H) = \frac{P(H \mid F)\, P(F)}{P(H \mid F)\, P(F) + P(H \mid \text{not } F)\, P(\text{not } F)} = \frac{0.291 \times 0.234}{0.291 \times 0.234 + 0.076 \times 0.766} = 0.540 , \]

exactly the proportion we counted. This is Bayes’ theorem (Bayes 1763), and it is nothing more than counting the people who share the evidence and asking what fraction of them have the condition.

Definition 2.6 (Bayes’ theorem; prior and posterior) For an event \(D\) (for example, a disease) and evidence \(E\) (for example, a test result),

\[ P(D \mid E) = \frac{P(E \mid D)\, P(D)}{P(E)}, \qquad P(E) = P(E \mid D)\,P(D) + P(E \mid \text{not } D)\,P(\text{not } D). \]

\(P(D)\) is the prior probability, before the evidence; \(P(D \mid E)\) is the posterior probability, after it; \(P(E \mid D)\) is the likelihood of the evidence under \(D\). In words: posterior ∝ likelihood × prior.

The same rule works for parameters: put a prior distribution on \(\theta\), multiply by the likelihood of Section 2.3, and you obtain a posterior distribution for \(\theta\). That is Bayesian learning of model parameters, the subject of Chapter 8. Here we stay with the case that matters most at the bedside: a test result.

2.4.2 The anatomy of a diagnostic test

Definition 2.7 (Prevalence, sensitivity, specificity, predictive values) For a condition \(D\) and a test with result \(+\) or \(-\):

  • Prevalence \(\pi = P(D)\): the proportion with the condition in the population tested; for one patient, the prior probability.
  • Sensitivity \(\text{Se} = P(+ \mid D)\): the proportion of people with the condition who test positive.
  • Specificity \(\text{Sp} = P(- \mid \text{not } D)\): the proportion of people without it who test negative.
  • Positive predictive value \(\text{PPV} = P(D \mid +)\): the proportion of positive results that are true.
  • Negative predictive value \(\text{NPV} = P(\text{not } D \mid -)\): the proportion of negative results that are true.

Sensitivity and specificity describe the test: they are measured in people whose true status is known (Altman and Bland 1994a). The predictive values describe what a result means for the patient in front of you, and they depend on the prevalence (Altman and Bland 1994b). Bayes’ theorem links them:

\[ \text{PPV} = \frac{\text{Se}\,\pi}{\text{Se}\,\pi + (1-\text{Sp})(1-\pi)}, \qquad \text{NPV} = \frac{\text{Sp}\,(1-\pi)}{\text{Sp}\,(1-\pi) + (1-\text{Se})\,\pi}. \qquad(2.6)\]

Back to Ward 7. Urine biomarkers of kidney stress have been studied as early warnings of AKI in critically ill patients (Kashani et al. 2013); we use an illustrative test with sensitivity 0.85 and specificity 0.8. For Tomás, whose prior risk is 0.07, Equation 2.6 gives a PPV of 0.24: even after a positive result, Tomás is more likely not to develop AKI. This is what you found by counting in the gate. For Aoife, with a prior of 0.49, the same positive result means a risk of 0.80. The same test, the same result, two very different conclusions, because the two patients started from different priors.

2.4.3 Natural frequencies

People, including doctors, find Equation 2.6 hard to apply. In a classic study, physicians and students at a Harvard hospital were told that a disease had a prevalence of 1 in 1 000 and that a test had a false-positive rate of 5 %, and asked for the chance that a person with a positive result had the disease; the most common answer was 95 %, while the correct answer is about 2 % (Casscells, Schoenberger, and Graboys 1978). A replication more than thirty years later found much the same (Manrai et al. 2014). Bayesian reasoning becomes far easier when the numbers are expressed as natural frequencies (counts of people, as in the gate) instead of conditional probabilities (Gigerenzer and Hoffrage 1995; Gigerenzer et al. 2007).

Likelihood ratios: Bayes’ theorem in one multiplication

Clinicians often summarise a test by its likelihood ratios, \(\text{LR}^{+} = \text{Se}/(1-\text{Sp})\) and \(\text{LR}^{-} = (1-\text{Se})/\text{Sp}\) (Deeks and Altman 2004). Writing probabilities as odds, \(\text{odds} = P/(1-P)\), Bayes’ theorem becomes \(\text{posterior odds} = \text{prior odds} \times \text{LR}\). For our test \(\text{LR}^{+} =\) 4.25: a positive result multiplies the odds of AKI by about 4.3. Aoife’s prior odds are 0.49/0.51 = 0.96, so her posterior odds are 0.96 × 4.25 = 4.08, a probability of 4.08/5.08 = 0.80. The word likelihood is not a coincidence: the ratio compares how well “AKI” and “no AKI” explain the observed result.

2.5 From posterior to decision: expected loss and thresholds

A posterior probability is information, not a decision. To decide, Emma and Seán must weigh the probability against what is at stake. Figure 2.2 shows the whole chain: a prior from a model, a test result, Bayes’ theorem, a posterior, and a comparison with a threshold set by values.

Definition 2.8 (Loss and expected loss) A loss is a number that says how bad a consequence is, relative to the best possible consequence in the same situation. The expected loss of an action is the average of its losses, each weighted by the probability of the state of the world that produces it. A rational choice under uncertainty takes the action with the smallest expected loss or, equivalently, the largest expected value (Howard 1966a; Hunink et al. 2014).

Tonight there are two kinds of mistake. Starting the bundle for a patient who would not have developed AKI is a false positive decision, with loss \(C_{\text{FP}}\): drugs paused, nursing time used, antibiotics possibly under-dosed. Giving standard care to a patient who then develops AKI is a false negative decision, with loss \(C_{\text{FN}}\). The right decisions have zero loss. With probability of AKI \(\theta\), the expected losses are

\[ \mathbb{E}[\text{loss} \mid \text{bundle}] = (1-\theta)\, C_{\text{FP}}, \qquad \mathbb{E}[\text{loss} \mid \text{standard care}] = \theta\, C_{\text{FN}} . \]

The bundle is better when \(\theta\, C_{\text{FN}} > (1-\theta)\, C_{\text{FP}}\), that is, when \(\theta\) exceeds the threshold

\[ t = \frac{C_{\text{FP}}}{C_{\text{FP}} + C_{\text{FN}}} . \qquad(2.7)\]

This is the threshold approach to clinical decisions (Pauker and Kassirer 1980). It is the same threshold you found with the sliders of Chapter 1 (Section 1.5). There, the team scored the four consequences 90 (bundle, no AKI), 100 (standard care, no AKI), 60 (bundle, AKI) and 30 (standard care, AKI). A loss is the gap to the best choice for the same state: without AKI, the bundle costs \(C_{\text{FP}} = 100 - 90 =\) 10 points; with AKI, standard care costs \(C_{\text{FN}} = 60 - 30 =\) 30 points. Equation 2.7 gives \(t = 10/(10 + 30) =\) 0.25, the value the widget showed. Notice what the threshold depends on: only the ratio of the two losses, not the model and not the test. The worse a missed AKI is compared with an unnecessary bundle, the lower the threshold.

Many software packages turn a predicted probability into a “yes” when it exceeds 0.5. According to Equation 2.7, what does that default silently assume?

2.5.1 Should we order the test? The value of information

Now combine the pieces for tonight’s three patients (Table 2.2). For each, compare the decision without the test with the decisions after each possible result.

The table tells three different stories.

  • Tomás (prior 0.07) would receive standard care without the test. A positive result raises his risk to 0.24: almost exactly at the threshold, but still below it. Even after a positive result standard care remains (just) the better choice (its expected loss is 7.27 points against 7.58 for the bundle), and a negative result confirms it. No result changes his management.
  • Aoife (prior 0.49) would receive the bundle without the test. A negative result lowers her risk to 0.15, below the threshold, and spares her the bundle.
  • Priya (prior 0.99) receives the bundle whatever the result. For her the test is wasted: information that cannot change the decision has no value.

The last three columns put a number on this. The expected loss with the test averages the best decision after a positive and after a negative result, weighted by how likely each result is. The difference is the expected value of information of the test (Howard 1966a, 1966b): for Aoife 1.87 points, for Tomás 0.00, for Priya 0.00. The test is worth ordering only if its own cost (money, delay, the burden of a false alarm) is smaller. This is the promise made in Chapter 1: information is valuable only when it can change a decision.

2.6 Entropy: uncertainty as surprise

We have measured how likely things are. It is also useful to measure how uncertain we are, in one number. Start with surprise. If an event has probability \(P\), its surprise is

\[ s = -\log_2 P , \]

measured in bits. An event that was certain (\(P = 1\)) carries no surprise; a coin landing heads (\(P = 0.5\)) carries 1 bit; an event with probability 1/8 carries 3 bits. Rare events are surprising, and surprise adds up over independent events because probabilities multiply.

Definition 2.9 (Entropy) The entropy of a random variable is its expected surprise: the average of \(-\log_2 P(x)\) over its possible values, weighted by their probabilities (Shannon 1948; Cover and Thomas 2006). For a yes/no outcome with probability \(\theta\),

\[ H(\theta) = -\theta \log_2 \theta - (1-\theta) \log_2 (1-\theta) . \]

It is 0 bits when the outcome is certain and reaches its maximum, 1 bit, when both outcomes are equally likely.

Aoife’s outcome has an entropy of 1.00 bit; Tomás’s 0.37; Priya’s 0.08. Like the value of information, entropy puts Aoife first: the patient near a coin toss is the one for whom more information is most likely to help. On average, the urine test reduces Aoife’s entropy by 0.33 bits; this average reduction is called the information gain of the test (Cover and Thomas 2006).

Entropy also connects back to likelihood. The negative log-likelihood of a dataset, divided by the number of patients and measured in bits, is the average surprise of the observed outcomes under the model. Maximising the likelihood, as in Section 2.3, is the same as making the data as unsurprising as possible. For the Ward 7 outcomes, a model that gives everyone the same risk of 0.58 leaves an average surprise of 0.98 bits per patient, almost the 1 bit of a coin toss. A model that gives each patient their own risk can do better, and how much better is a measure of how much it has learned. This average surprise is called cross-entropy (Goodfellow, Bengio, and Courville 2016, ch. 5). It is the loss used to train the classifiers of Chapter 5, it is the “entropy” that decision trees reduce when they choose a split in Chapter 6, and it is the quantity that trains the language models of Chapter 16, where the outcome is not AKI-or-not but the next word.

The model of Chapter 5 gives Tomás a risk of 0.07 and Priya a risk of 0.99. Whose outcome is more uncertain according to entropy?

2.7 Where this fits in the decision loop

Which decision does this chapter support? Every decision in which an uncertain outcome matters: whether to start Aoife’s bundle, whether to order a test, how many renal reviews Seán should plan for. Probability is how a model hands information to a decision-maker, Bayes’ theorem is how new evidence updates it, and the expected loss is how it meets values.

What can go wrong? A sensitivity read as if it were a predictive value; a test result interpreted without the patient’s prior; a probability estimated from 20 patients treated as if it came from 1 250; a threshold chosen by default (0.5) instead of from the consequences; tests ordered whose results cannot change anything.

Who stays in control? The model supplies \(\theta\); the test supplies a likelihood ratio; but the losses \(C_{\text{FP}}\) and \(C_{\text{FN}}\), and therefore the threshold, come from the patient and the clinicians. Change the values and the same probability leads to a different decision, as it should.

Selected answers

1. \((1-\hat\theta)^{10} = (1 - 0.58)^{10} \approx\) 0.00017. Real patients differ in risk, and patients on the same ward may share causes (the same outbreak, the same nephrotoxic drug policy). Heterogeneity and shared causes both make extreme counts (very few or very many cases) more likely than the binomial model suggests, so planning on the binomial can underestimate bad weeks.

3. \(L(\theta) = \theta^3(1-\theta)\): \(L(0.5) = 0.0625\) and \(L(0.75) \approx 0.105\), so 0.75 explains the data better. But the likelihood curve for four patients is very wide: every value between about 0.3 and 0.98 has at least a fifth of the maximum likelihood. A decision should not rest on four patients.

4. Of 100 000 people, 100 have the disease and all test positive; of the 99 900 without it, about 4 995 test positive. PPV = 100/(100 + 4 995) ≈ 0.02: about 2 %, not 95 %.

6. \(t = 1/(1 + 10) \approx 0.09\). Aoife (0.49) and Priya (0.99) are above it and receive the bundle without a test; Tomás (0.07) is just below it and receives standard care. With this lower threshold a positive test (0.24) would now change his management, so for him the test has value.

8. Swapping \(\theta\) and \(1-\theta\) swaps the two terms of the sum. Entropy measures how spread the probability is over the two outcomes, not which outcome is labelled “yes”.

2.10 Summary

  • A random variable has a distribution: Bernoulli for one yes/no outcome, binomial for a count of yes’s, normal for many measurements. Its expectation is the long-run average and its variance the spread. A density is not a probability; areas are.
  • The likelihood measures how well each parameter value explains the observed data; the maximum likelihood estimate is the best-explaining value (\(k/n\) for a proportion, the sample mean for a normal mean). A wide likelihood means little data.
  • Bayes’ theorem updates a prior into a posterior: posterior ∝ likelihood × prior. For a test, sensitivity and specificity describe the test; PPV and NPV describe what a result means and depend on prevalence. Natural frequencies make the calculation transparent.
  • A probability becomes a decision through expected loss: act when the probability exceeds \(t = C_{\text{FP}}/(C_{\text{FP}} + C_{\text{FN}})\). Information has value only if it can move a patient across the threshold.
  • Entropy measures uncertainty as expected surprise; average surprise of observed outcomes (cross-entropy) is the loss that trains classifiers and language models.
