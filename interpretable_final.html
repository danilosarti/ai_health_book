10 Introduction

In previous sections of the book, we learned how to perform supervised learning techniques and how to predict continuous or categorical outcomes using explanatory features and models such as OLS, LASSO, Ridge, Elastic Net, decision trees, random forests, XGBoost, and neural networks.

In this chapter, we examine how interpretability techniques help us understand not only the predictive performance of such models, but also what their parameters, structures, and prediction patterns mean. In addition, for models whose internal structure is more complex—such as random forests, gradient boosting models, Bayesian trees, and neural networks—we introduce techniques that help open the black box and translate model behavior into forms more accessible to human reasoning.

11 Motivational example

For this chapter, we use a small simulated lung cancer dataset designed for teaching interpretability. The dataset is inspired by the classical lung dataset from the survival package, but it is fully simulated, complete, and computationally lightweight. It contains 228 patients and includes a small set of clinically interpretable predictors together with two outcomes.

The primary continuous outcome is time, representing a survival-related time scale in days. We also include an auxiliary binary outcome, status_bin, which indicates whether the patient belongs to a higher-risk outcome group. In this chapter, our main modeling target is the continuous outcome time, since it allows us to compare a wide range of supervised learning models using a common regression framework.

The predictors are a small set of clinically meaningful covariates: age, sex, ph.ecog (ECOG performance status), ph.karno (physician-rated Karnofsky score), and wt.loss (weight loss). The data-generating mechanism was constructed so that some effects are approximately linear, while others are nonlinear or interaction-based. In particular, worse ECOG performance status and greater weight loss are associated with shorter predicted survival time, while better Karnofsky scores are associated with longer expected time. The simulated structure also includes an interaction between ph.ecog and wt.loss, creating a useful setting in which additive models, tree-based methods, and model-agnostic interpretability tools may reveal different aspects of the same predictive problem.

This dataset is intentionally small enough to allow coefficient-based interpretation, tree visualization, partial dependence analysis, and VIVI-style summaries to run quickly and reproducibly in a teaching environment. At the same time, it remains rich enough to illustrate key interpretability ideas such as variable importance, structural summaries, interaction effects, and the distinction between model-dependent and model-agnostic explanations.

12 The importance of interpretability

12.1 Interpretable AI

Interpretability in artificial intelligence refers to the capacity to render the behavior of a predictive model intelligible to human agents. In scientific, medical, and regulatory settings, predictive accuracy alone does not suffice. Models are embedded in inferential arguments, decision processes, and institutional accountability structures. Their outputs must therefore be accompanied by representations that allow examination, comparison, and reasoned critique.

Interpretability is relational and not a purely intrinsic property of an algorithm. A model is interpretable relative to a given audience, a specific decision context, and a defined epistemic objective. What constitutes an adequate explanation in methodological research may differ from what is required in clinical practice or policy evaluation. For this reason, interpretability must be understood simultaneously as a technical problem and as a problem of structured communication. Interpretability functions as a bridge between technical and non-technical personnel, as shown in Figure 12.1.

12.2 The context of communication

Every explanation presupposes a communicative structure. There is a system that generates signals, a representation of those signals, and an agent who interprets them within a conceptual framework. A predictive model encodes relationships between predictors and outcomes in a formal structure. However, this structure is typically not expressed in a form directly accessible to human reasoning. It may be embedded in a large collection of trees, a high-dimensional parameter vector, or a posterior distribution over functions.

Interpretability methods function as translation mechanisms. They construct secondary representations of a trained model in order to expose selected aspects of its internal structure or external behavior. These representations do not reproduce the full complexity of the model. Instead, they selectively preserve features such as predictor relevance, interaction strength, marginal effects, structural stability, or uncertainty.

From the perspective of the fundamental problem of communication, no representation is transmitted without transformation. A model encodes structure in mathematical or algorithmic form; interpretability techniques decode and re-encode that structure into forms compatible with human conceptual systems. The adequacy of an interpretability method depends on whether it preserves the structural features that are epistemically relevant in the context of use.

12.3 Scientific communication

In scientific practice, models serve not only as predictive instruments but also as representational frameworks. A linear regression expresses relationships through coefficients under explicit distributional and structural assumptions. A logistic regression encodes associations in terms of log-odds. A decision tree expresses structure through recursive partitioning of the predictor space. Each modeling framework carries an implicit theory of explanation grounded in its parameterization and assumptions.

Classical statistical models were often selected in part because their parameters possessed direct interpretative meaning. With the development of modern machine learning algorithms—such as random forests, gradient boosting machines, and neural networks—the emphasis shifted toward predictive performance in complex and high-dimensional settings. As model structure became more intricate, interpretability increasingly required dedicated methodological development. Models became black boxes, and the aim of this chapter is to make the main interpretive strategies explicit, including their assumptions, their limitations, and the kinds of representational objects they produce.

Interpretability procedures may therefore be regarded as meta-modeling operations. They take a fitted model as input and derive structured summaries of its behavior under controlled perturbations or structural inspection, often through visual representations designed to support scrutiny and comparison.

12.4 Models and representations as communication

Formally, a supervised learning model can be described as a function

\(g : \mathcal{X} \rightarrow \mathcal{Y}\),

where \(\mathcal{X}\) denotes the predictor space and \(\mathcal{Y}\) the response space. In practice, the learned mapping is rarely available in closed analytical form. Instead, it is encoded in computational structures: regression coefficients, weighted sums, collections of trees, boosting iterations, neural layers, or posterior samples.

Interpretability techniques construct alternative representations of this mapping. These may emphasize the relative influence of predictors, the presence of interaction effects, the shape of marginal response functions, or the stability of structural features across model realizations. Each such representation abstracts from certain aspects of the original mapping while preserving others. The design of interpretability methods therefore involves explicit trade-offs between fidelity to the fitted model, robustness to sampling variability and noise, computational feasibility in high dimensions, and cognitive clarity for a target audience.

A useful way to formalize this point is to view interpretability as producing a representation \(\mathcal{R}\) from a model \(g\) and data \(D\) through a transformation operator \(\mathcal{T}\):

\(\mathcal{T}(g, D) \mapsto \mathcal{R}\).

The representation \(\mathcal{R}\) can take multiple forms, including scalar summaries (importance scores), structured objects (interaction matrices), functional summaries (marginal effect curves), or geometric objects (networks, embeddings of proximity structure). The evaluation of \(\mathcal{R}\) depends on whether it supports the epistemic objective at hand—explanation, auditing, validation, or decision support—given the literacy level and symbolic logic available to the audience.

13 Model-Dependent and Model-Agnostic Methods

Interpretability methods are commonly classified into two categories: model-dependent (embedded) and model-agnostic (Figure 13.1).

Model-dependent methods exploit the internal structure of a specific algorithm. They derive explanations from how the model is constructed and parameterized. Model-agnostic methods, by contrast, treat the fitted model as a black box and rely exclusively on its predictive behavior under controlled input variation.

This distinction reflects two complementary interpretative strategies. One examines structural components; the other examines functional behavior. Structural strategies tend to be computationally efficient and tightly aligned with the model class, while functional strategies are more broadly applicable but can be computationally intensive and sensitive to the data-generating structure, especially when predictors are correlated or constrained.

13.1 Model-Dependent Methods

13.1.1 Linear Models: OLS, LASSO, Ridge, Elastic Net

Model-dependent interpretability is most transparent in linear models, because the fitted structure is directly expressed through a coefficient vector. In this setting, each coefficient represents the expected change in the outcome associated with a one-unit increase in a predictor, holding the remaining predictors fixed. Since the predictors were preprocessed consistently, the coefficient magnitudes can also be compared as relative measures of influence within the fitted model.

13.1.1.2 Penalized linear models: LASSO, Ridge, and Elastic Net

We now compare this unpenalized baseline with three regularized linear models: LASSO, Ridge, and Elastic Net. These methods preserve the same basic additive structure, but they modify the estimation procedure through penalization. Ridge shrinks all coefficients continuously toward zero, LASSO can set many coefficients exactly to zero, and Elastic Net combines both mechanisms.

This sequence of outputs illustrates the essence of model-dependent interpretability in linear settings. The explanatory structure is encoded directly in the coefficient vector, but its final form depends strongly on the fitting procedure. OLS offers the clearest unpenalized additive baseline. Ridge yields a dense but stabilized representation. LASSO produces a sparse explanatory narrative focused only on the strongest predictors. Elastic Net lies between these two extremes, retaining the dominant variables while allowing limited additional structure. Together, these models provide an interpretable bridge between classical regression and more flexible predictive methods.

This coefficient-based perspective is especially powerful when the internal structure of the model is itself meaningful, as in linear and regularized linear regression. However, many modern predictive models do not expose their logic through a compact parameter vector. In those settings, interpretability must often be constructed from model behavior rather than read directly from model structure, motivating the move to tree-based summaries and model-agnostic methods.

13.2 Decision trees

13.2.1 Recursive partitioning as explanation

Beyond linear models, decision trees provide another important example of model-dependent interpretability. In a regression or classification tree, the fitted model is expressed as a sequence of recursive partitioning rules applied to the predictor space. Each internal node corresponds to a split on one predictor, and each terminal node corresponds to a predicted outcome for observations satisfying the sequence of conditions along that branch.

This makes single trees intrinsically interpretable. Unlike linear models, where interpretation is carried by a vector of coefficients, trees encode explanation through a rule-based structure. A fitted tree can be read as a hierarchical set of conditional statements: if a patient satisfies one condition, the model follows one branch; otherwise it follows another. In this sense, the tree itself becomes an explanatory object. Its structure reveals which variables are used first, which thresholds define meaningful partitions, and how the predictor space is divided into clinically distinct subgroups.

In the lung cancer example used in this chapter, a regression tree may begin by splitting patients according to performance status (ph.ecog) or weight loss (wt.loss), two variables that strongly influence predicted survival time. Subsequent splits may refine the prediction using variables such as Karnofsky score (ph.karno), sex, or age. These partitions define subgroups of patients with different expected survival profiles. Variables appearing in early splits often play a particularly influential role in the partitioning strategy of the tree, although this should not be confused with a formal global measure of variable importance.

Tree ensembles such as Random Forests, Gradient Boosting, and Bayesian Additive Regression Trees extend this idea by aggregating large numbers of trees. Individual trees in these ensembles are usually not interpreted directly, because the predictive model is distributed across hundreds of trees. Instead, interpretation relies on structural summaries derived from the ensemble, such as variable importance measures based on split frequency, impurity reduction, or prediction degradation under variable perturbation. These summaries remain model-dependent because they arise directly from the internal architecture of the fitted tree-based model.

At this stage it is useful to distinguish these embedded structural summaries from model-agnostic interpretability techniques. Some interpretability tools can operate in both settings. For example, the vivid framework combines measures of variable importance and interaction strength that can be derived either from model-specific structures or from model-agnostic procedures such as permutation and partial dependence. Later in this chapter we will examine these approaches in more detail. For now, the focus remains on the structural interpretability naturally provided by single trees and tree-based models.

13.2.3 From embedded importance to model-agnostic functional summaries

Embedded importance measures derived from tree ensembles provide useful structural summaries of how predictors contribute to model construction. These summaries reveal which predictors were most influential in building the ensemble, but they do not directly show how predictions change as individual predictors vary across their observed ranges. In other words, variable importance identifies which variables matter for prediction, but it does not explain how those variables influence the predicted outcome or whether their effects remain stable across different observations.

To investigate these functional relationships we turn to model-agnostic interpretability methods. These techniques treat the fitted model as a prediction function and study how predictions respond to controlled changes in the input variables. Because they rely only on the prediction mechanism and not on the internal structure of the model, these methods can be applied to any supervised learning algorithm, including tree ensembles and neural networks.

In the analyses that follow, we introduce a sequence of model-agnostic interpretability tools that progressively reveal different aspects of model behavior. We begin with permutation-based measures of global variable importance. We then examine partial dependence plots and individual conditional expectation curves, which describe how model predictions vary as predictors change. Finally, we return to the VIVI framework, which combines importance and interaction structure into a unified representation.

These techniques allow us to move from structural summaries of model construction toward functional explanations of model behavior. In the present example they are applied to three predictive models with increasing structural flexibility: a Random Forest, an XGBoost model, and a Neural Network. Together, these visualizations provide a richer picture of how the clinical predictors influence predicted survival time in the simulated lung cancer dataset.

13.2.4 Global variable importance

13.2.4.1 Permutation importance

Permutation importance provides a model-agnostic way to quantify how strongly a fitted predictive model relies on each predictor variable. The central idea is simple: if a predictor is important for prediction, then breaking the association between that predictor and the outcome should substantially degrade predictive performance.

Let \(\hat{g}\) denote the fitted prediction function and let \(w(\hat{g}, D)\) represent a performance score computed on dataset \(D\), such as RMSE, MAE, or classification error. For a predictor \(X_r\), we construct a perturbed dataset \(D^{\pi_r}\) by randomly permuting the values of \(X_r\) while leaving all other predictors unchanged. The fitted model is then evaluated on this perturbed dataset.

Repeating the permutation \(R\) times yields the permutation importance score

\[ imp_r = w(\hat{g}, D) - \frac{1}{R} \sum_{k=1}^{R} w(\hat{g}, D^{\pi_r,k}) \]

This quantity measures how much predictive performance deteriorates when the information contained in predictor \(X_r\) is destroyed. Large values indicate that the model relies heavily on that predictor, whereas values close to zero suggest that the predictor plays a limited role in the predictive mechanism.

Permutation importance has several advantages. Because it relies only on the prediction function \(\hat{g}\), it can be applied to any supervised learning model, including tree ensembles, boosting models, and neural networks. It also allows a direct comparison of variable importance across different algorithms within the same predictive task.

However, permutation importance must be interpreted carefully when predictors are correlated. Permuting one variable may disrupt the joint dependence structure between predictors, creating combinations of feature values that are rare or unrealistic in the observed data. In such cases the resulting scores should be interpreted as measures of model reliance rather than as causal measures of variable influence.

13.2.5 Interaction diagnostics

Variable importance describes how strongly individual predictors contribute to prediction, but it does not reveal whether predictors act independently or jointly. Many predictive models capture interaction effects, meaning that the influence of one predictor depends on the value of another.

Model-agnostic interaction diagnostics often rely on partial dependence functions. Let \(f_r(x_r)\) and \(f_s(x_s)\) denote the univariate partial dependence functions for predictors \(X_r\) and \(X_s\), and let \(f_{rs}(x_r,x_s)\) denote the bivariate partial dependence surface. If the model is additive in these variables, the joint effect can be approximated by the sum of the marginal effects.

A widely used interaction diagnostic is the Friedman–Popescu \(H\)-statistic, defined as

\[ H_{rs}^2 = \frac{ \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left[ f_{rs}(x_{ir},x_{is}) - f_r(x_{ir}) - f_s(x_{is}) \right]^2 }{ \sum_{i=1}^{n} f_{rs}(x_{ir},x_{is})^2 } \]

Large values of \(H_{rs}\) indicate strong departures from additivity, suggesting that the predictive effect of one variable depends on the value of another. Interaction diagnostics therefore complement variable importance by revealing whether predictive influence arises mainly through marginal effects or through joint relationships among predictors.

13.2.5.1 VIVI: Variable importance and interaction structure

The VIVI framework provides a joint representation of variable importance and interaction strength. Instead of examining these components separately, the method summarizes them in a matrix-like visualization in which diagonal elements represent the marginal importance of each predictor, while off-diagonal elements represent the strength of pairwise interactions between predictors.

Unlike embedded importance measures derived directly from tree-based algorithms, VIVI can be computed in a model-agnostic way. The method relies on partial dependence calculations to estimate both marginal effects and interaction structure, allowing the same interpretability framework to be applied across very different modeling approaches.

In the present example we compute VIVI summaries for the Random Forest, XGBoost, and Neural Network models. This comparison allows us to examine how each model distributes predictive influence across the clinical predictors and whether interactions between predictors play a substantial role in the prediction of survival time.

The VIVI representations show a highly consistent predictive structure across the three fitted models. In all cases, the variables ph.ecog, wt.loss, and ph.karno form the main core of the model, appearing with the largest marginal importance values. This indicates that patient functional status and clinical deterioration are the dominant drivers of predicted survival time in the simulated lung cancer example. By contrast, age and sex_male appear with substantially lower importance, suggesting a more limited role in the predictive structure.

The interaction patterns are also informative. Across Random Forest, XGBoost, and Neural Network models, the strongest pairwise interactions are concentrated among ph.ecog, wt.loss, and ph.karno. In the Random Forest and XGBoost models, the interaction between wt.loss and ph.karno appears especially strong, while the interaction between ph.ecog and wt.loss is also consistently prominent. In the Neural Network, the interaction structure is slightly more centered on ph.ecog, which acts as a highly important variable connected strongly to both wt.loss and ph.karno. Taken together, these results suggest that the fitted models agree not only on the most important individual predictors, but also on the fact that prediction depends on their joint action rather than on purely additive effects.

13.2.6 Partial dependence and individual conditional expectation

Interpretability methods based on variable importance and interaction summaries provide information about which variables contribute most strongly to prediction. However, these summaries do not directly reveal the functional relationship learned by the model between predictors and the outcome. In other words, they identify the most influential variables, but they do not show how predicted values change as those variables vary. To understand this functional behavior, we turn to model-agnostic techniques that analyze the response surface of the fitted model itself. Two of the most widely used tools for this purpose are Partial Dependence Plots (PDP) and Individual Conditional Expectation (ICE) curves.

Consider a predictive model \(\hat{f}(\mathbf{x})\) trained on a set of predictors \(\mathbf{x} = (x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_p)\). Suppose we wish to understand the effect of a subset of predictors \(\mathbf{x}_S \subseteq \mathbf{x}\), where \(S\) indexes a set of variables of interest, and let \(\mathbf{x}_C\) denote the remaining predictors. The partial dependence function is defined as the expected model prediction when the variables in \(S\) are fixed at specific values and the remaining predictors vary according to their empirical distribution:

\[ f_S(\mathbf{x}_S) = \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_C}\left[\hat{f}(\mathbf{x}_S, \mathbf{x}_C)\right]. \]

Because the joint distribution of predictors is typically unknown, this expectation is approximated empirically using the observed dataset. Given \(n\) observations, the partial dependence function can be estimated as

\[ \hat{f}_S(\mathbf{x}_S) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \hat{f}(\mathbf{x}_S, \mathbf{x}_{C,i}), \]

where \(\mathbf{x}_{C,i}\) denotes the observed values of the complementary predictors for observation \(i\). Intuitively, this computation evaluates the model prediction repeatedly while fixing the variable of interest at a chosen value and averaging over all observed combinations of the remaining predictors. By repeating this process across a grid of values for \(\mathbf{x}_S\), we obtain the partial dependence curve, which describes the average predicted response as the predictor varies.

Partial dependence therefore provides a population-level view of the model’s learned relationship between predictors and the outcome. The resulting curves reveal whether the model encodes approximately linear relationships, monotonic nonlinear patterns, or more complex functional shapes. Importantly, PDPs do not depend on the internal structure of the model and can therefore be applied to linear models, tree ensembles, neural networks, and other predictive algorithms.

However, partial dependence curves represent an average effect across all observations. When predictors interact strongly with other variables, averaging may conceal substantial heterogeneity in the individual responses. To address this limitation, Individual Conditional Expectation (ICE) curves examine the model prediction separately for each observation.

For a given observation \(i\), the ICE function is defined as

\[ \hat{f}^{(i)}_S(\mathbf{x}_S) = \hat{f}(\mathbf{x}_S, \mathbf{x}_{C,i}), \]

which represents the predicted response when the predictor of interest varies while all other predictors are fixed at the values observed for that specific individual. Plotting this function for all observations produces a family of curves, each describing how the model prediction would change for a particular observation as the predictor varies.

ICE plots therefore reveal heterogeneity in predictor effects across the dataset. When the individual curves are roughly parallel, the predictor has a relatively homogeneous effect across observations, and the partial dependence curve provides a reliable summary of the relationship. When the curves diverge or intersect, the effect of the predictor depends on the values of other variables, indicating the presence of interactions.

The VIVI analysis revealed that the predictive structure of the models is dominated by three variables: ph.ecog, wt.loss, and ph.karno. These variables represent different aspects of patient functional status and physical deterioration, and they appear not only as the most important predictors across the Random Forest, XGBoost, and Neural Network models, but also as the variables most strongly involved in pairwise interactions. Having identified these key predictors, the next step is to examine how each of them influences the predicted outcome across its range of values.

While variable importance and interaction summaries describe which variables matter for prediction, they do not reveal how the prediction changes as those variables vary. Partial dependence plots address this question by describing how the model prediction changes, on average, as one predictor varies while the remaining predictors are averaged over their empirical distribution. In this way, PDPs provide a functional description of the learned relationship between predictors and the outcome.

In the present lung cancer example, partial dependence analysis focuses on ph.ecog, wt.loss, and ph.karno, allowing us to visualize how each model translates these clinical variables into predicted survival time. Because the same predictors are examined across Random Forest, XGBoost, and Neural Network models, the resulting curves also allow a comparison of how different algorithms approximate the same predictive relationship.

However, partial dependence curves summarize the average relationship across all observations. When predictors interact, averaging may hide important variation in the individual responses. Individual Conditional Expectation (ICE) curves address this limitation by plotting the predicted response for each observation separately as the predictor varies. Instead of a single average curve, ICE plots show a family of curves corresponding to individual patients.

When the ICE curves are roughly parallel, the predictor has a relatively homogeneous effect across observations and the partial dependence curve provides a good summary. When the curves diverge or cross, this suggests that the effect of the predictor varies depending on the values of other variables, indicating the presence of interactions. In the present case, this is particularly relevant because the VIVI analysis already indicated strong interactions among ph.ecog, wt.loss, and ph.karno.

Together, PDP and ICE analyses provide a complementary perspective on the fitted models. PDPs reveal the overall functional relationship learned by the model, while ICE curves reveal how that relationship varies across individuals. This combination allows us to move from structural summaries of the model toward a more detailed understanding of its predictive behavior.

13.2.7 Generalized pairwise partial dependence plots

The univariate partial dependence and ICE plots examined above describe how the model prediction changes when a single predictor varies while the remaining predictors are averaged over their empirical distribution. These displays are useful for identifying the marginal functional form associated with each variable, but they do not directly reveal how pairs of predictors act jointly in the fitted response surface. When interpretability requires understanding interactions among predictors, multivariate visual summaries become necessary.

The vivid package provides a graphical tool designed for this purpose: the generalized partial dependence pairs plot, produced by the function pdpPairs(). This visualization extends the logic of univariate partial dependence by representing several layers of information within a single matrix-style display.

In these plots, the diagonal panels contain univariate partial dependence curves together with ICE trajectories, allowing each predictor to be examined individually. The upper triangular panels display bivariate partial dependence surfaces, which describe how the predicted response varies when two predictors change simultaneously while the remaining variables are averaged over their empirical distribution. These surfaces make it possible to detect interaction patterns such as curvature, threshold effects, or regions where the influence of one variable depends on the value of another. Finally, the lower triangular panels display the empirical distribution of the observed predictors, helping to assess whether the regions shown in the partial dependence surfaces are well supported by the available data.

In the present lung cancer example, this visualization is particularly useful because the previous VIVI analysis suggested that ph.ecog, wt.loss, and ph.karno form a strongly interacting set of predictors. The generalized pairs plot therefore allows us to study the joint structure of the learned response surface across the Random Forest, XGBoost, and Neural Network models.

The generalized pairwise partial dependence plots combine several complementary visual summaries in a single matrix display. The upper triangular panels display bivariate partial dependence surfaces, where the color scale represents the predicted value of the response variable across combinations of two predictors. These surfaces allow the analyst to detect interaction patterns, such as whether the effect of one variable becomes stronger or weaker depending on the value of another variable. Finally, the lower triangular panels show the empirical distribution of the predictors in the observed dataset. These panels provide an important reference because they indicate which regions of the predictor space are actually supported by the data and which regions correspond to extrapolation of the fitted model.

Although the general structure is consistent across the three models, some differences in smoothness and geometry can be observed. The tree-based models—Random Forest and XGBoost—produce surfaces that appear more piecewise and segmented, reflecting the recursive partitioning structure of tree ensembles. Taken together, these plots provide a coherent interpretation of the fitted models. 13.2.9 Zen Partial Dependence Plots

While generalized pairwise partial dependence plots provide a comprehensive view of bivariate predictor relationships, they become increasingly difficult to read as the number of predictors grows. In a model with \(p\) predictors, the number of possible pairwise displays increases as \(p(p-1)/2\), which can quickly make full pairwise partial dependence grids visually impractical.

To address this problem, the vivid package provides Zen Partial Dependence Plots (ZPDPs) through the function pdpZen(). This visualization is based on the concept of zenplots, introduced by Hofert and Oldford, as a compact strategy for exploring many pairwise relationships in high-dimensional settings. Rather than displaying every possible pairwise panel, the method selects and orders the most relevant variable pairs and arranges them in a zig-zag sequence that preserves readability while emphasizing interpretively important structures.

Formally, let \(X_1, \dots, X_p\) denote the predictors in a fitted model \(\hat{f}\), and let \(I_{jk}\) denote a symmetric measure of pairwise interaction strength between predictors \(X_j\) and \(X_k\), such as the interaction values derived from the VIVI matrix. A Zen Partial Dependence Plot may be understood as a graph-based visualization built on a weighted undirected graph

\[ G = (V, E, W), \]

where each vertex in \(V\) corresponds to a predictor, each edge in \(E\) corresponds to a pair of predictors, and the edge weights \(W_{jk}\) are given by the interaction strengths \(I_{jk}\).

The purpose of the zen layout is to construct a traversal path through the graph that prioritizes edges with larger weights, so that the displayed sequence of bivariate partial dependence plots focuses on the most important pairwise relationships in the model.

This idea is inspired by Eulerian path constructions in graph theory. In an Eulerian traversal, one visits edges of a graph in a systematic order. Zenplots adapt this principle to statistical graphics: instead of drawing all pairwise plots in a matrix, they construct a path through the most relevant variable pairs and display the corresponding bivariate partial dependence surfaces sequentially. In this way, the visualization becomes not only more compact, but also more explicitly guided by interaction strength.

For a given pair of predictors \(X_j\) and \(X_k\), the displayed bivariate partial dependence surface is based on the estimated function

\[ \hat{f}_{jk}(x_j, x_k) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \hat{f}(x_j, x_k, \mathbf{x}_{C,i}), \]

where \(C\) denotes the set of all remaining predictors and \(\mathbf{x}_{C,i}\) are the observed values of those remaining predictors for observation \(i\).

Each panel of the Zen plot therefore displays the average fitted response over a grid of values of \((x_j, x_k)\) while averaging over the empirical distribution of the other predictors.

The key difference between a generalized pairwise PDP matrix and a Zen PDP is therefore not the mathematical definition of the bivariate partial dependence surface itself, but the selection and ordering of the displayed predictor pairs. The generalized pairwise PDP aims at exhaustive display over a selected subset of variables, whereas the Zen PDP aims at structured prioritization, concentrating visual attention on variable pairs that are most relevant according to the model’s interaction structure.

In practice, this makes Zen Partial Dependence Plots especially useful in settings where the predictor space is moderate or high dimensional and where a full matrix of pairwise PDPs would either exceed the available plotting space or dilute attention across too many low-information panels. In such cases, the Zen layout acts as a navigational device through the model’s most relevant interaction surfaces.

A particularly useful feature of the Zen approach is that the traversal path can be defined explicitly from the interaction structure previously estimated by the VIVI framework. In this chapter, this creates a coherent interpretability pipeline: first we estimate variable importance and pairwise interaction strength using vivi(), then we use those interaction values to determine which bivariate partial dependence surfaces deserve visual emphasis.

This makes the Zen PDP especially valuable from a didactic perspective, because it does not merely show a compact collection of pairwise PDPs. 13.3 Bayesian Additive Regression Trees and uncertainty-aware interpretability

Up to this point, most of the interpretability tools discussed in this chapter have been either model-dependent summaries for classical models or model-agnostic summaries applied to flexible predictive algorithms. This strategy is useful because it allows a common interpretive language across very different model classes. However, one important advantage of Bayesian machine learning models is that they do not provide only point predictions or point summaries of importance. Instead, they provide posterior distributions over predictive structures.

This motivates a final shift in the chapter. Rather than asking only which variables are important, how predictions vary across the predictor space, or how interactions appear on average, we now ask how stable these patterns are under posterior uncertainty. Bayesian Additive Regression Trees (BART) are especially useful in this setting because they represent the fitted function as a sum of many small trees while retaining a full posterior distribution over the model.

In the present example, BART achieved the best predictive performance among all fitted models. This makes it a natural model with which to conclude the chapter, since it allows us to move from interpretability as point summary toward interpretability as structured uncertainty assessment. To explore this idea, we now turn to structural summaries derived from the fitted BART model using the bartMan framework.

13.3.1 Why BART is different

Bayesian Additive Regression Trees model the regression function as a sum of weak trees,

\[ f(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{j=1}^{m} g(\mathbf{x}; T_j, M_j), \]

where each \(T_j\) denotes a tree structure and \(M_j\) its associated terminal-node parameters. In the Bayesian formulation, priors are placed over trees and terminal node values, and inference proceeds through posterior sampling. As a result, the fitted model is not a single deterministic function but a posterior distribution over functions.

This has an important interpretive consequence. Many familiar machine-learning summaries, such as variable importance or partial dependence, are often presented as fixed point estimates. In BART, however, these quantities may be examined across posterior draws, allowing the analyst to distinguish large effects from stable effects and to evaluate how uncertain a given structural pattern may be.

13.3.1.1 Fitting a Bayesian Additive Regression Trees model

In the previous sections of this chapter we examined several supervised learning algorithms and explored a variety of interpretability techniques, including coefficient analysis, tree structures, variable importance summaries, interaction matrices, and functional response plots such as partial dependence and ICE curves. We now turn to Bayesian Additive Regression Trees (BART), a model that combines the flexibility of tree ensembles with the uncertainty quantification provided by Bayesian inference.

Unlike many machine-learning algorithms that produce a single fitted model, BART represents the regression function as a posterior distribution over functions. Each posterior draw corresponds to one possible ensemble of trees consistent with the observed data. As a consequence, interpretability can be based not only on the central predictive function but also on the distribution of plausible models supported by the posterior.

In the following analysis we fit a BART model using the dbarts implementation. The fitted model produces posterior samples of predicted outcomes, posterior samples of the residual variance, and internal summaries describing how often predictors are used within the ensemble of trees. These components form the basis of an uncertainty-aware interpretability analysis.

13.3.4 Structural interpretability with bartMan

While posterior predictions and variable usage provide useful summaries of predictive behavior, they do not yet reveal the internal structure of the tree ensemble. To examine these structural properties we use the bartMan framework, which extracts tree-level information from the fitted BART model and provides a set of visual tools for exploring the ensemble.

The first step is to extract the tree structures from the fitted BART object. This representation allows us to compute inclusion-based variable importance, interaction summaries, and tree-structure visualizations.

Internally, the dbarts implementation stores the posterior ensemble of trees in a compact format optimized for MCMC sampling and prediction. While efficient for computation, this internal representation is not immediately convenient for structural interpretation. The function extractTreeData() performs this transformation. The resulting object contains the full set of trees across posterior iterations and forms the basis for several interpretability diagnostics. Each element of the matrix summarizes how frequently variables appear within the splitting structure of the trees and how often they co-occur along decision paths.

Consequently, the trees in a BART ensemble are typically shallow and simple, and their interpretation should be understood in the context of the ensemble rather than in isolation.

In the dendrogram representation, the root node shows the first partition of the predictor space, which in this case is based on weight loss (wt.loss). Observations with standardized weight loss below the threshold are sent to the left branch, while those above the threshold follow the right branch. The left branch is further partitioned using Karnofsky performance score (ph.karno), illustrating how the model refines predictions by combining multiple indicators of patient condition. The terminal nodes display small numeric values representing the contribution of that tree to the predicted outcome. These values are typically modest because the final prediction is obtained by summing contributions from many trees across the ensemble.

The icicle representation provides an alternative view of the same structure. Instead of focusing on hierarchical branching, the icicle plot emphasizes how the predictor space is divided into rectangular regions corresponding to the terminal nodes of the tree. Each block represents a region of the predictor space in which the model assigns the same local contribution to the prediction. The colors highlight these regions and help visualize how the tree partitions the space according to the splitting variables.

Taken together, these plots illustrate the local decision structure learned by one tree in the BART ensemble. Although each tree is simple, the aggregation of many such trees allows the model to approximate complex nonlinear relationships and interactions among predictors. Examining individual trees therefore provides insight into the basic building blocks of the model while reinforcing the idea that the predictive function emerges from the collective behavior of the entire ensemble.

13.3.7 VSUPS

While the standard VIVI matrix summarizes the average importance and interaction strength across the posterior ensemble, it does not explicitly represent the stability of these structural patterns. Two variable interactions may have similar average magnitudes but very different levels of variability across posterior draws.

To address this limitation, the bartMan framework provides the VSUP (Variable Selection Uncertainty Plot) representation. VSUP extends the VIVI matrix by incorporating a measure of uncertainty associated with each importance and interaction estimate. This allows the analyst to distinguish interactions that are consistently present across posterior draws from those that appear only sporadically.

In the code below, the VSUP matrix is computed using the argument type = "vsup". The option metricError = "CV" specifies that uncertainty is quantified using the coefficient of variation across posterior draws. Interactions with higher variability therefore appear with stronger uncertainty signals in the visualization.

The resulting plot encodes two dimensions simultaneously: the magnitude of importance or interaction and the stability of that quantity across the posterior distribution. This representation highlights structural relationships that are both strong and stable, providing an uncertainty-aware perspective on the internal structure of the BART model.

The distinctive feature of the VSUP representation is that it also encodes the uncertainty of these structural summaries. In this framework, interactions or importance measures that remain stable across posterior samples appear with stronger and more saturated colors, while relationships with greater variability appear more muted.
